# Chapter Five — The First Wing

A city is a kind of animal, and like any animal it can hold one great fear at a time and no more. Through the eleven days of the road and all the uncounted days since — the walk in the collar, the barring of the doors no one outside yet knew of, the sealed hush at the city's heart — the Free Cities had held the fear of the caged thing they had chained and led home and shut, with their own hands and their own iron, in the Hall of Nine Keys — and that fear had been terrible but it had at least been *theirs*, a thing they had chosen, a thing with a chain on it and a wall around it and, they had told themselves in the language ledgers understand, a price already paid. Now the beacon burned on the north wall and a second wyrm sat on the bald hill beyond the north gate, unchained, unwalled, uninvited, with no price paid on it at all, and the city discovered the thing every animal discovers when a second fear comes down out of the sky: that the first fear had been a comfort, because it had been the only one.

It did not know, yet, that its Nine were sealed inside the first fear and could not answer. It only knew that the sky had come down on the north hill, and that the men who ran the city — the deputy wardens, the guild-seconds, the captains left holding the wall while their betters sat behind bronze that would not open — were, every one of them, now the most important people they had ever been, and none of them wanted to be.

## What the Hill Held

By the second morning the whole city had climbed, in shifts and in dread, to look at it.

They came up onto the north wall the way people come to look at a body — quietly, in ones and twos, telling themselves they came to *assess*, which is the word the frightened use for *stare*. And what they saw did not help them, because what they saw did nothing. The second wyrm lay on the bald hill north of the gate with its true wings folded down along its flanks like a ship's sails struck and lashed, and its head laid low toward the city, and its furnace eyes half-lidded against the grey day, and it *waited*, with the same geologic patience the first one had shown in the road east of Ashford, the patience of a thing that has all the time there is and knows it. It did not stir toward the wall. It did not stir away. When the wind came round out of the north it carried the heat of the creature to them across the whole width of the killing-ground before the gate — a dry forge-warmth, wrong in the cold spring air, that made the sentries' eyes water and the horses in the gate-yard scream and would not settle — and that heat was the only message the hill sent, and no one could read it, and everyone tried.

The captains of the north wall did what captains do with a thing they cannot understand: they described it, in the hope that describing was a kind of controlling. They counted the length of it against the fence-lines of the bald hill and set it longer than the caged one, or shorter, depending which captain you asked and how much he had slept. They marked the color of its scales, which was the grey-going-to-iron of the northern cloud it had come out of, so that at a distance and in the flat light it was hard to say where the hill's own shoulder ended and the creature began — a thing built, someone said and wished he hadn't, *to be looked for and not found.* They timed its breathing, as Sera Lowe was timing the caged one's breathing at that very hour behind the bronze, neither knowing the other did it, the two measurers on either side of the wall taking the pulse of the two halves of the same enormous unanswerable question.

And in every account the captains sent down to the deputies, and the deputies sent round to one another in the long emergency sittings that were council in all but name and lawful authority, one question sat under all the others and would not be dislodged. It was the question the whole chapter of the city's fear now turned on, and it was this:

*What is it to the one we hold?*

## Master, Kin, or Lure

They argued it in the guild-halls and the gate-houses and the cold rooms behind the barred bronze where the deputies met and pretended to a power that had walked into the Hall of Nine Keys and never walked out. They argued it the way the Nine had argued the first wyrm's *nature* — reaching for the words they had, finding the words too small, and mistaking the smallness of the words for the smallness of the thing. And as the Nine's argument had collapsed at last into three lawful findings, the deputies' argument collapsed into three, and they were these.

Some said the second wyrm was **master** to the first: that the caged thing in the Hall was a scout, an envoy, a hostage even, sent ahead into the cities' arms to take their measure from the inside, and that now the true power had come to see what its creature had found, and to collect. This was the reading of the Wallsmen's deputies, the reading of men who understood the world as a chain of command, and it was the most frightening reading, because it made the cities' great capture into a small clever move in a game whose board they could not see — made the chain they had forged in nine days a *gift* the enemy had let them spend themselves to give. If the hill held the master, then the Hall held a spy, and the cities had sealed the spy inside their own seat of judgment with their own hands, and lit a beacon to tell the master his spy was safely delivered.

Some said the second wyrm was **kin**: mate, brood-mate, parent, child — the reading of the guild-women and the fen-folk and the old ones who remembered farm animals and grief, the ones who looked at the great grey thing laying its head low toward the city and saw not strategy but *waiting-for*, the terrible patient posture of a creature that has followed another to the door of a cruel house and will sit outside it until the world ends or the door opens. This was the reading that could not be argued down, because it explained the one thing the master-reading could not: why the hill-wyrm did nothing. A master collects. A master is impatient; a master, finding its envoy caged, burns the cage. But a thing that only *waits*, that lays its head toward the wall and lets its heat blow uselessly across the killing-ground and does not come — that is not a master. That is a mourner. And a city can chain a master, or try to. A city does not know what to do with a grief the size of a hill.

And some — fewer, and quieter, and none of them willing to say it in a full sitting — said the second wyrm was neither, but a **lure**. That the two of them, caged and free, hall and hill, were the two halves of a single working; that the point of the whole slow terrible pageant, the flight toward the breach, the head lowered for the collar, the eleven-day walk into the judgment-hall, was never the caged wyrm at all but the *cities* — that the creatures had come, patiently, deliberately, the way you bait a trap you are in no hurry to spring, to gather the frightened clever animals into one place, around one well, under one roof of bronze, and *hold their attention*, north and center at once, while something the cities had not thought to watch for did the thing it had come to do. The lure-readers could not say what that something was. They only felt, the way the young outrider had felt at the clean cold hollow in the Ashford ash, that the danger was not the thing everyone was looking at. That the two wyrms, the one they held and the one they could not, were a pair of hands cupped around the city's face, turning it gently to look north, and south, and never down.

None of the three readings could be proven. All three were true. This is the particular horror of the thing that was happening to the Free Cities: they had a mystery with a body they could measure, and every measurement they took only told them the size of what they did not understand.

## What a God Requires

Behind the bronze, in the temple that had been a hall, the eight who could not flee were learning the answer to the question the city outside had not even thought to ask — not *what is it,* which they had each settled now in the private ruin of their own naming, but *what does it want.*

The answer came slowly, and it came the way weather comes, which is to say it was a change in the air before it was a thing anyone could point to. In the first shapeless days the wyrm in the well had simply *attended*, as it had attended the council — banked, patient, folded into the marble like smoke poured backward into a bottle, requiring nothing, taking nothing, giving nothing but the dry forge-heat that would not let them rest. And in that requiring-nothing they had almost, in the animal way, begun to hope: that a god that asked for nothing might in time be *nothing*, might be endured, might be a weather they could learn to live inside. Peryn of the Archive knew better, and said so, once, and then held his peace, because it is cruel to take a hope from people who have nothing else, and crueler still that the taking makes no difference — the hope goes anyway, in its own time, and all your warning bought was that they hated you for the little while they had left to hope.

What the wyrm required, they learned, was **attention**. Not worship in the shape the old founders would have known it — no blood, no burnt offering, no chant. Simpler than that, and worse. It required to be *looked at*. It required to be the thing the room was *for*. And this it did not demand, because a god does not demand; it merely arranged, by the fact of its presence, that nothing else in the room could hold the eye for long.

Hedric of the Wallsmen learned it first, being a man built to watch for threats. He had paced the perimeter of the hall for days, counting its weaknesses out of the habit that was all he had left, and he found — measuring one dead afternoon how long he could keep his eyes on the murder-holes above the barred door, on the honest ordinary stone of a defense he understood — that he could not. That his eye slid off the stone and down into the well the way water slides downhill, the way iron slides toward the lodestone, not by his choosing and against it. That he could give the door perhaps a hundred count of true attention and then the well had him again, the furnace eyes had him, and he was watching the god watch him, and the door and the wall and the whole trade of his life had gone grey and small at the edge of his sight. *This* was what a wallsman could not build against. Not a thing that broke the wall. A thing that made the wall not worth looking at.

Sera Lowe learned it in the ruin of her measuring. She had written the creature down — length and heat and the interval of its breath — because measurement was her wall, the scrivener's wall of number against the numberless; and she found, going back over her own pages by lamplight, that the measurements had *changed her hand.* That the writing had begun as a cage for the creature and become, page by page and without her leave, a devotion — that she was no longer recording a specimen but *keeping a record of a presence,* the way the founders' archivists had once kept the records of the reflecting well, and that somewhere in the turning she had stopped writing to understand and started writing to be *near.* She read the last true measurement she had taken — *the eye regarded me* — and understood that it was not the last thing she had failed to measure. It was the first thing she had worshipped.

And Vessa of the River Guilds, who had gone silent, who had sat with her back to the unmovable bronze and watched the well like a fire she was no longer sure she had lit — Vessa learned it in the silence, which was the only offering she had left to make. She who had named the world to master it had nothing left to name; she who spent every word like a resented coin had spent her last; and in the long attending silence she found the terrible ease of it, the rest of it, the animal relief of a creature that has finally stopped fighting the current and let the river have it. It was not peace. She would have struck any man who called it peace. But it was the end of struggle, and to a woman who had struggled all her life against ledgers that would not balance and doors that would not open, the end of struggle wore, in the bad light of the temple, very nearly the face of peace, and that was the most frightening thing that had happened to her yet — more frightening than the doors, more frightening than the burning she had not seen and only heard of — that she had begun, in the sealed and lampless days, to be *grateful* to the thing that had taken everything, for taking, at last, the burden of resisting it.

This was what a god required. Not their deaths. Not their prayers. Their *attention,* freely and then helplessly given, until attention became habit and habit became need and need became, in the human animal that cannot bear for long to fear a thing it does not also love, the beginning of devotion. The wyrm in the well did nothing to them at all. It simply stayed, and was worth looking at, and let the looking do the rest — the way water does nothing to stone but stay, and be lower, and let the staying do the rest.

Only Peryn kept his eyes, and Peryn kept his eyes because he had already given the creature the one thing it wanted, early and whole, in a single sentence in the argument before the doors — *one surrenders to be recognized* — and having recognized it he owed it no more attention than one owes a fact. He watched the others slide instead. He was the archivist of their falling, as Sera was the archivist of the creature's stillness, and he made, in his head, where no bronze could seal it and no god could read it, the record he could not write: that a god is not a thing that takes your worship. A god is a thing you cannot stop giving it to. And the difference between those two sentences was the whole difference between the old law's *sovereign,* which is a thing you treat with, and Aldric's *god,* which is a thing you become the temple of, and the founders had thrown away the word for exactly that difference because it could not be legislated, and now the nine of them were living inside the space where the word used to be.

## The Boy, and the Small Thing Stirring

At the north gate, through all of it, the boy of the night-watch kept his own hours and his own counsel.

He was there when the shifts of the fearful came up onto the wall to stare at the hill, and he did not go up with them, because his fear was not on the hill. It was under his own two hands, in the warm swept hollow in the ash by the gate, where the small breathing patient thing had lain quiet since the night the column brought the great one home — the thing no ledger recorded, that had followed the caged wyrm east one night's travel back all the eleven days, and that only he had knelt to, in the cold, in the dark, where the whole city's eye was turned the other way. He came to it on every watch and some that were not his. He brought it nothing, because it required nothing; he only knelt by it, and kept it company, and was kept company by it, and told no one, because there were no words in the new law for what passed between them and he did not know the old ones and had begun, in the way of children, to make his own.

He called it, in the private speech he made for it and never spoke aloud, *the one that is coming.* Not *the egg,* which was a thing you cooked, and not *the wyrm,* which was the great grey terror on the hill and the greater one under the Hall; but *the one that is coming,* because that was the truest thing he knew about it and the only thing that mattered — that it was not yet, and would be, and that of all the frightened clever animals in the Free Cities he alone was there to wait for it, the way the grey thing on the hill was waiting for the grey thing in the Hall, the way, though he could not have said it, everything in the whole slow terrible working was waiting for everything else.

And on the night the argument in the guild-halls reached its highest and most useless pitch — the night the deputies at last resolved, in the careful language of people who have learned that saying nothing is the safest vote, to *watch and wait and send to the other cities for counsel,* which is to say to do nothing while calling it prudence — on that night, kneeling in the dark at the gate with the beacon still red on the north wall above him and the heat of the hill-wyrm blowing faint and dry across the killing-ground, the boy felt the ash move under his hands.

It was not much. It was the smallest thing that happened in the Free Cities that night, and the largest. A shifting, deep in the warm swept hollow — a settling and a re-settling, the way a sleeper turns toward morning — and then, against his palms, through the ash, a single slow pressure outward, patient and enormous for so small a thing, the pressure of something *testing the size of its dark.* And with it, rolling up through the boy the way it had rolled up through him twice before, the deep struck-bell note, the note the great bar makes dropping into its bronze cradle — but changed now, layered, no longer one toll but the three he had heard on the night of the beacon, the three struck notes finding each other under the floor of the world: the low far one from the sealed Hall at the city's heart, and the near far one from the hill in the north, and, between them and beneath his own two hands, the third — closer than either, and rising, and no longer answering the other two but *calling* them, the way the youngest voice in a round comes in last and turns two notes into a chord and the chord into a thing that was always going to be sung.

The boy bowed his head over the warm ash. He did not run. He had never once run, from the burned meeting-hall of Ashford to the gate of the Free Cities, and he did not run now. He only knelt, and kept his counsel, and waited with the one that was coming, while above him and around him the whole enormous animal of the city held its one great fear at a time — the wing on the northern hill — and never once thought to look down, at the gate, at the ash, at the small stirring hand of the thing that had come all this way not to be caught, and not to be caged, and not to be judged, but simply, in the fullness of the patient terrible time it had all the time there was to keep, to be *born.*

---

*So the city learns its second fear and cannot name it; and the eight in the temple learn what a god requires and cannot refuse it; and at the gate the one that is coming stirs against the boy's two hands and calls the far notes home. In the sixth chapter the fracture inside the sealed hall comes at last — for a room of nine cannot attend a god forever without one of them breaking the attending; and the word of the kneeling, which the barred bronze has kept from the city that does not yet know it was ever spoken, begins to find the cracks in the door. The instrument under the floor of the world plays on, three notes going toward a fourth, and no council in the Free Cities is convened, or could be, to hear it.*
