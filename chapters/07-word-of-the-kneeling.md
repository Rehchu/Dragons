# Chapter Seven — Word of the Kneeling

There is an old word in Ashford — was, before Ashford became a field of grey shapes — that the founders' law never touched, because it was too small and too poor to be worth throwing away. The law threw away the great word, the old-tongue name for what the wyrms are, the one Peryn of the Archive kept the empty shelf of; but the law let the little word alone, the way a magistrate who has burned a man's library will not trouble to burn the songs his kitchen-women sing, because songs are not writing and kitchen-women are not the record. And so the little word lived on where the great one died: not in the Hall, not in the ledgers, not in the mouths of wardens, but in the hearth-tongue, the speech that has no letters, the speech a grandmother uses to a frightened child over a banked fire on a cold night — *hush now, or the ember-drake will hear you turning in the ash.*

That is what they called it in Ashford, the thing that slept in the ground under the meeting-hall: not a wyrm, which was a word for a thing you might see, a thing on a hill or in a well; but an **ember-drake**, which was a word for the fire that does not go out. The hearth-tongue knew what the law had forgotten and the founders had thrown away — knew it not as doctrine but as the thing you tell a child, which is the last place true things go to hide when the clever have finished with them: that under the floor of the world there is a burning, old past counting, that was here before the town and would be here after, and that it is not dead, and is not asleep the way you are asleep, but banked — *banked, child, the way I bank this fire, so it will not go out in the night and will not burn the house down while we sleep, so it will be there in the cold grey of the morning, waiting, red under the grey, to be woken.* And they were not wrong, the grandmothers of Ashford, in any particular but one. They thought the banking was theirs. They thought a thing you tell a child is a thing you control.

The morning the ember-drake woke, there were no grandmothers left in Ashford to hear it. There was only a boy, at a gate a long day east, with his two hands in a warm hollow in the ash, and he did not know the old word, and he would learn it before he learned any other, because it was the truest thing left in the world and the world was about to say it out loud.

## The Deputies' Lie Goes Out

Begin above the ground, where the clever are, because that is where the noise is.

The shadow-council of deputies — the men and women who ran the city with the Nine sealed under its heart — had done the thing deputies do, which is to write down a lie and seal it in grey-and-iron wax and send it out by rider to keep the world the shape it was yesterday. *Deny the rumor of any irregularity in the deliberations of the Nine. The council sits in ordinary session and will render its lawful verdict in ordinary time.* Nine riders went out on nine roads on nine tired horses, and the wax on the letters was still soft when the first of them met, coming the other way, a thing the deputies had not thought to write a lie against: the beacon-fires.

For the beacon-chain was lit — had been lit since the wing came down on the northern hill — and a beacon does not lie, cannot lie, has only the one thing it can say, which is *a wyrm, a wyrm, look north.* And so on every one of the nine roads that night a rider carrying *all is ordinary* passed, in the dark, going the opposite way, the red word of a beacon burning *all is not.* The riders saw the fires. They could not help but see them; a beacon is built to be seen. And each rider, carrying under his coat the sealed wax that swore the council sat in calm and lawful session, looked up at the mountain-tops burning one after another into the west and the east and felt, in the place under the place where a man keeps his orders, the first cold suspicion that he was the courier of a lie — that he had been handed the wax not because it was true but because someone above him needed it to be believed, and had chosen him to do the believing on the country's behalf.

This is the first thing to understand about the word of the kneeling, and why no rider and no wax could outrun it: it did not need to be *said.* It needed only to be *possible.* The country did not receive the rumor and the denial and weigh them like a magistrate weighing two depositions. The country received a beacon that could not lie and a letter that had every reason to, arriving together, in the dark, on the same road; and the country did what frightened countries do, which is not to reason but to *lean* — to lean, all of it, all at once, the way a field of grain leans before a wind you cannot yet feel, toward the worse of the two things it has been told. By the time the sealed wax reached the far magistracies and was read aloud in their council rooms in the flat voices of clerks, the leaning had gone before it. The clerks read *all is ordinary* to rooms that already knew, in the leaning way, that nothing was; and the reading did not calm the rooms. It confirmed them. For there is nothing that persuades a frightened people a thing is true like being officially told it is not.

## The Word Runs the Country

It ran the way it had run the city — ear to ear, the oldest engine there is — but now it had the whole plain to run across, and it changed as it ran, the way a name changes going down a line of children until the last child says a word the first would not know.

In the city it had been three plain sentences carried by a foreman named Corse: *they say the council did not judge it. They say a warden knelt. They say it is a god.* By the time it had crossed the first day's country it was no longer three sentences. It was a shape, a lean, a thing you did not say but arranged your day around — the market-women setting their stalls a little nearer the walls, the carters taking the long road that did not pass under the north beacon, the temple-less finding, some of them, that their feet took them toward the sealed heart of the city in the grey before dawn and standing there, not praying, for there was nothing to pray to that they could see, only *facing* — facing the direction of the thing, the way a plant in a dark room turns its leaves to the one crack of light without knowing it turns, without knowing there is such a thing as light, knowing only *this way, not that.*

The deputies felt it and could not name it, the way the city had felt the second wyrm as a thickening in the northern cloud before it was a shape. They felt the fear of the place change temperature. It had been a clean cold fear — the fear of a caged beast, sharp and simple, the fear you can post guards against and issue proclamations to. Now there was a warmth coming into it, a wrongness, and the deputies, who were not stupid, understood the warmth was the worst thing that had ever come into their city, worse than the wyrm on the hill, worse than the wyrm in the well: for a cold fear wants the danger *gone,* and can be governed by promising to remove it; but a warm fear does not want the danger gone. A warm fear wants to *go to* the danger. A warm fear is the beginning of worship, and there is no proclamation against worship, no guard you can post at the door of a leaning heart.

So they wrote another letter. They always write another letter. And this one they did not send by rider on the open roads where the beacons could shame it, but quietly, hand to hand, to the other deputies of the other cities, deputy to deputy in the shadow-tongue of men who run things while the named rulers are away — a letter that did not deny the rumor, because denial had failed, but did the cleverer and older thing: it *renamed* it. *A superstition is abroad among the low people. In their fear they have made a god of the beast. This is the known sickness of the commons in a time of omen, and the cure is the known cure: firmness, ordinary business, and the swift lawful verdict of the Nine, which will show the thing to be a beast and break the fever.* And the deputies of the other cities read this and were grateful for it, because it gave them something to do that was not leaning — it gave them a way to be *above* the fear instead of in it — and they set about, in nine cities, being firm and ordinary and grateful, and did not notice that they had, all nine of them, just agreed in writing that the thing under the Hall might be a god, and had only disagreed about who was allowed to say so.

That is how the word won. Not by being believed. By being *argued against.* You cannot argue against a thing without holding it in your mouth to do it, and a word held in the mouth has already half gone down.

## The Ground Keeps Its Accounts Out Loud

Now go down, under the noise, to where the true thing was happening — under the Hall, under the streets, under the leaning market-crowds and the firm grateful deputies, down through the founders' stone and the older stone under that, down to where the little word came from.

The older burning was awake.

It had never, in the strict sense, been asleep — the hearth-tongue had that right; it was banked, not out — but it had lain for ages under Ashford and under the plain and under the roots of the Hall itself in the long red patience of a thing with no reason to hurry, threaded down through the bones of the world like a seam of buried coal that is also, the miners say and will not explain, a *root.* And when the first wyrm rose from it at Ashford and burned the garrison and flew east toward its cage; and when the great chain was forged and the collar set and the thing led into the Hall built, though no one living had known it, *over another finger of the same seam;* and when Aldric knelt and named it and the doors barred and the eight began to be worn smooth in the temple that had been a hall — through all of that, the older burning had been doing the one thing it did, the thing the ground had begun to do since the Arising and would not now stop doing: it had been keeping its accounts out loud.

You could feel it, if you were the sort of creature that feels such things and not the sort that reads them. The engineers coring the door-frame felt it and thought it was the drills. The beacon-watchers on the north wall felt it and thought it was the cold. The horses felt it and would not stand quiet in the stables near the Hall, and the dogs felt it and had left the sealed quarter days ago, going out through the gates in the grey with their tails low and not coming back, and no one had marked the dogs, because a city that has learned to fear does not notice the smaller animals learning first. Under the streets, in the seam, the fire kept its count — not in words, for it had none, and not in the founders' lost name for itself, for a fire does not know its own name any more than you know the name a doctor would give the beating in your chest; it kept its count in *pressure,* in the slow enormous arithmetic of a banked thing beginning to draw air, and the sum it was working toward, ages in the summing, was very near done.

For a fire that is banked is not waiting to burn. It is waiting to be *needed.* And under the Hall of Nine Keys, in the well the founders built to read the future in still water, a thing had come at last that needed it — a thing chained and named and worshipped and worn, that had let itself be caught, that had convened a council to judge it and had been, in the deep of the sealed dark, judging back; and the older burning, the ember-drake of the hearth-tongue, the last and oldest fire, felt the need of it come down through the stone the way warmth comes down a wall from a fire in the room above, and answered the only way a fire answers, which is to *reach.*

## What Woke Beneath the Gate

But the seam did not reach first toward the Hall, though the Hall was nearest and the chained thing was there. It reached toward the gate.

Toward the gate, because that was where the *new* thing was — the small warm thing in the ash, the egg the boy kept his hours over, the one that is coming. The seam under the world was old past counting and the thing in the well was old, but the thing in the ash was *young,* was the youngest fire there had ever been, was the seam's own reaching made small enough to be born; and the older burning, keeping its accounts out loud, drawing its slow air, reached under the plain the long day's distance from Ashford to the city gate the way a root reaches toward water it cannot see, not because it decided to but because that is what the fire was *for* — and came up, at last, not in a burning, not in a rising, not in a thing the walls could beacon or the deputies deny, but as a warmth in the ground under a boy's two hands, deepening.

The boy felt it change. He had felt the notes all along — the far low one from the Hall, the far near one from the hill, the close warm one under his palms, and then the fourth that had come in under all of them in the deep of the city's worst night and gathered them into one sounding thing. He had felt the one that is coming stir and settle and press outward, testing the size of its dark. Now he felt something else, something the four notes had been the tuning-up of, and it was not a note at all. It was the ash going *hot.*

Not hot to burn. He did not snatch his hands back; he had never once, in all his nights, snatched his hands back, and he did not now. It went hot the way a stone goes hot that has sat all day in a sun you did not notice was on it — a low, deep, arriving heat, coming up not from the egg alone but from *under* the egg, from under the gate-stones, from under the road and the wall and the killing-ground and the whole grey country between him and the fallen town where his grandmother, had he had one still, would have known the word for what was happening and would have whispered it to him over a fire: *hush now. The ember-drake has heard you. The ember-drake is awake.*

The boy did not have the word. But he had his two hands in the warm ash, and he felt, coming up through the ground into his palms and his knees and the bones of his face bent low over the hollow, the thing the whole slow terrible working had been for: the last fire, the oldest and the newest at once, the banked thing that had waited under the floor of the world since before there were floors — waking. Not rising. Not yet. Waking. Turning, in its ash the way a sleeper turns toward a morning it has felt before it has seen, drawing its first full breath in an age, and beginning — the boy understood this with his hands, the way he understood everything, and did not run — beginning to *come up.*

Above him, red on the wall, the beacon burned its one honest word into the dark. Behind him, through the sleeping streets, the other word ran ear to ear, *a god, a god, we have chained a god,* leaning the frightened city toward its own sealed heart. And under him, under all of it, under the boy and the gate and the leaning city and the lying deputies and the worn temple and the chained god in the founders' well, the older burning drew its long breath and kept its accounts out loud, and the ground itself began, very slowly, to warm.

The boy took his hands out of the ash — the first time, in all his nights, that he had taken them out not because his watch was ended but because the ash had told him to — and he stood, and he looked, not north at the wing, not back at the city, but *down,* at the warm hollow glowing faintly now in the dark like the eye of a banked fire, and he said the only thing there was to say, in the plain child's speech that was all he had, not knowing he was the last of Ashford and the first of whatever came after, not knowing he had just become the thing the grandmothers had been for:

"You're waking up," he said. "I felt you. You're waking up."

And under the ground, the ember-drake, that had no ears and no name and no need of either, kept coming up.

---

*So the deputies' lie meets the beacon that cannot lie, and the country, given two truths on the same road in the dark, leans toward the worse one; and the word of the kneeling runs the whole plain ear to ear, winning not by being believed but by being argued against, until nine cities have agreed in writing that the thing might be a god and disagreed only about who may say so. But the true event is under the noise, in the seam of the world: the older burning — the **ember-drake** of the Ashford hearth-tongue, the little word the law was too proud to throw away — draws its long breath at last and reaches, not toward the chained god in the well but toward the youngest fire, the one that is coming under the boy's two hands at the gate; and the ground begins to warm. In the eighth chapter the reaching must arrive: the nine cities, each in its own fear, must answer a word already loose among them, while beneath the gate the last ember-drake keeps coming up, and the boy who does not run stands over a hollow that has begun, faintly, to glow.*
