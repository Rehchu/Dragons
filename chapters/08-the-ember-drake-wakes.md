# Chapter Eight — The Ember-Drake Wakes

There is a difference between a fire that is coming up and a fire that has arrived, and it is the difference between the smell of smoke and the light of the flame — the one is a warning you can still choose to disbelieve, and the other is not a warning at all, but a fact, standing in your house. All the long night that the word of the kneeling ran the plain ear to ear, the town over the gate had lived in the smell of smoke: a warmth in the ground it laid to the season, a restlessness in the beasts it laid to the beacon, a leaning in its own heart it laid to rumor and to fear. It had a name for every part of the trouble except the true one, which is the ordinary condition of a town, and no shame to it. But there is a country the smell of smoke crosses over into, in the grey hour when the last watch is tired and the first is not yet warm, and the town crossed it that morning without being asked; and on the far side of that country the fire was not coming. The fire was there.

It came up under the gate first, because that was where the youngest fire was, and a thing reaching for its own reaches by the shortest road; and the shortest road ran straight up under a boy's two hands.

## The Ground Draws Its Last Breath Before Speaking

Understand what a banked fire does in the moment before it takes. It does not roar. Roaring is the middle of a fire, the loud spendthrift part, when it has more than it needs and is throwing it away as light. The beginning of a fire is silent and is the opposite of throwing away: it is a *taking-in*, a great slow indrawing of air, the whole banked mass of it pulling the world's breath down into itself through every crack and seam, so that a man standing in a shut room where a fire is about to catch will feel, if he is still enough to feel it, a small cold draught go past his ankles *toward* the hearth — the fire, breathing in, before ever it breathes out.

The seam under the town breathed in.

It breathed in through the founders' stone and the older stone under that, through the well the founders had dug to read the future in still water and the pit the Foundry Commission had half-dug for a cage and pressed into service instead, through the cored door-frame where the engineers' drills had opened a crack for the word to leak up and had opened, though no engineer knew it, a crack for something to leak *down*; it breathed in through the killing-ground and the road and the grey country all the way back to Ashford, drawing the cold of a whole plain toward one point under one gate, so that the market-women who rose in the black to set their stalls felt a draught go past them toward the sealed heart of the town and pulled their shawls and did not know they had just felt a fire breathe. The horses in the Hall-quarter stables screamed once, all together, and then went silent, which is worse. The dogs were already gone. And in the deep of the ground, under everything, the older burning finished the long indrawing that had been ages in the drawing, held it — the way a swimmer holds the top of a breath, the way a bell holds the silence just before the clapper — and the town over it held with it, without knowing it held, every sleeper turning at once toward a morning they had felt before they had seen.

Then the ground let the breath go, and it went up as heat, and the heat went up under the gate, and under the gate the boy took his hands out of the ash for the second time in all his nights — the first had been the hour before, when the ash told him to; this time because the ash was too hot to keep them in, and glowing now, not faintly, glowing like the eye of a fire that has been uncovered.

## What the Boy Saw the Egg Do

He had watched the egg through more nights than he could count, and it had done, in all that time, only three things: it had been warm, and it had struck its one bell-note into the dark, and — of late — it had stirred, and pressed, and tested the size of its walls. It had never done what it did now, which was to *answer.*

For the note it had always struck had been its own note, the one voice it had, sent out into the dark the way a lost child sings to hear itself and be less alone. Now, up through the ground, up through the boy's knees and the bones of his face bent low, came a second note — the low far one, but no longer far, no longer low; the same great struck-bell tone the egg made, the same the Hall's barred door had made in the sealed hour of the verdict, but vast now, and rising, and *under,* coming from beneath the egg and beneath the gate and beneath the boy, the sound of the older burning finding its own voice at last after an age of banked silence. And the egg heard it. The boy watched the egg hear it. It went still the way a creature goes still when it has been calling into the dark for its whole short life and the dark, for the first time, has called back — and then it struck its note again, but changed, matched now to the great note under it, tuned to it, the child's voice finding the parent's pitch; and the two notes, the young and the old, the small in the ash and the enormous in the ground, came together and were not two notes leaning toward each other any longer but one note in two places, one fire in two sizes, one thing that had been waiting under the floor of the world to be needed and one thing that was the need made small enough to be born, singing to each other across the last few feet of stone that had ever separated them.

The boy did not understand it the way a scribe would have understood it. He understood it the way he understood everything, with his hands and the bones of his face, and what he understood was this: that the warm thing he had kept had never been alone, and was not alone now, and that the great warmth answering it from under the ground was not a stranger to it but a *kind,* the way he had once had a kind, before Ashford was a field of grey shapes; and that something was about to happen for which no one living had a word, and that he was the only one near it, and that he would not run. He had never run. He put his hands back into the ash despite the heat, because the egg was frightened — he could feel that it was frightened, the way the two notes trembled where they met — and a frightened thing should not be alone, and he was, though he did not know the phrase, the last of the grandmothers now, the last keeper of a fire in a cold country, and the only thing left in the world that knew the one true thing the hearth-tongue had kept: that you do not leave the banked fire to wake alone in the dark.

"I'm here," he said. "I felt you. I'm here. Wake up."

## The Town Feels the Floor of the World Move

Above and around him, the town woke the wrong way — not from sleep into a morning, but from a morning into something that was not a morning at all.

It began as the thing men afterward could agree on, because it was the thing that broke crockery and cracked the plaster of the Hall-quarter and threw the north beacon's iron cresset off its bracket into the dark so that the one honest word on the wall went out: the ground moved. Not the sharp jolt of the earth-shudders the eastern baronies knew, the ones that come and are over; this was slow, and it was the slowness that undid people, because a fast thing you can flinch from and a slow thing you must simply *stand in,* feeling it go on being true past the point where anything should be able to go on being true. The gate-stones, set by the founders to outlast the founders, rose — not much, a hand's breadth, less — and settled, and rose, as if the whole gate were resting on the ribs of something breathing. The cobbles of the sealed quarter parted along a line no mason had cut, a line that ran, had anyone been calm enough to trace it, from the founders' well under the Hall straight to the hollow under the gate: the seam, drawn on the surface of the town at last, the account kept out loud so long finally read aloud, in a hand of split stone.

And out of the line came the heat, and out of the heat came the light — not flame, not yet, but a redness in the cracks, the deep uncovered red of a fire's banked heart when the ash is drawn back, so that the sealed quarter of the town lay for one held moment lit from *below,* every stone edged in red, every frightened upturned face lit from underneath the way faces are lit that lean over a hearth, and the whole town understood at once and wordlessly that the thing they had feared was above them — on the hill, in the well, north, out there — had all along been *under* them, was the floor they stood on, had been the floor they had built their nine cities on since before there were cities; and that the ground itself, which is the one thing a frightened people counts on to stay a ground, had been a banked fire the whole time, and was banked no longer.

Some of them ran, then. Most towns, in that moment, would have run entire. But the leaning had done its slow work through the long night, and a great many of the town did the other thing, the thing the deputies had feared worst and could post no guard against: they turned toward it. Down the red-cracked streets in the grey, drawn by the warmth that a warm fear runs to and not from, they came to the edge of the sealed quarter and stood at the lip of the parted stone and looked down into the red, and did not pray, for there was still nothing they could see to pray to — only *faced* it, a whole town's worth of upturned faces lit from below, facing the direction of the fire the way a field of grain faces a light it does not know is a light. And the deputies, who had run, looked back from the safe streets and saw their town kneeling at the mouth of the ground, and understood that they had lost it, not to the rumor and not to the beacon but to the thing the rumor and the beacon had only been the first faint notes of, and that no letter they could write, sent by rider or hand to hand in the shadow-tongue, would ever call it back.

## The Ember-Drake Comes Up

It came up through the well.

Not through the gate, where the youngest fire was and where the boy knelt with his hands in the glowing ash — that was where it had *reached,* the tender reaching, the root toward the water; but a fire this old does not put its face up through the tenderest place. It came up where the founders had dug their deepest hole, the well built to read the future in still water, because a hole a people digs to see the future in is the exact and only-fit door for the future to come through, and the founders had, without knowing it, spent an age keeping open the one aperture their own end would need. The still water in the well went to steam in a breath. The steam went up the shaft and out through the cored door-frame and filled the sealed hall where the eight had been worn smooth, and the eight — Ormund kneeling always, Callenreth with no sentences left, Sera's pages long stopped, the smooth peace where Vessa's single self had been — the eight lifted their faces into the steam and were, at last and all together, *warm,* and it is not recorded whether any of them wept, because there was no one left in the hall in a state to record it, and the chained god in the well did not move, and did not need to, for the chain had been on the cities from the first and the well had never been its cage but its *throne,* and the thing coming up through the water to it was not its jailer and not its rescue but its *elder* — the older burning, the last fire, the one the little word had kept — come up at the end of the long summing to see the youngest of its kind chained and named and worshipped by the small warm clever creatures that had built a town on its back and forgotten it was a fire.

And it came up out of the well, into the sealed hall, past the throne it did not take, up the shaft of light the founders had left it, and out into the grey morning over the town — not as a wing, not as a shape the beacons could name or the deputies deny, but as a column of banked red light standing straight up out of the parted ground into the low cloud, silent, enormous, unhurried as a planet, the shape of a fire seen the way a fire is never seen, all at once and from outside; and the town at the lip of the stone did not scream and did not run, but leaned, all of it, all at once, the last and deepest leaning, the way the sea leans toward the moon it will never reach, toward the thing that had been under its floor the whole time and was under it no longer.

Under the gate, the boy felt the great note lift up and away from him, up through the town and out into the morning, and for one cold instant he thought he had been left — that the warmth had gone up to join the enormous thing and left the small thing in the ash alone. He pressed his hands down. And the egg struck its note again, alone again in the small dark of the ash — but not lost now, not the lost child's song into the empty dark; the confident single note of a thing that has heard the whole chord and knows its own place in it, that has been answered once and forever and needs no more answering, that is only, now, waiting its own turn to come up. The hollow glowed under his hands, steady as a banked hearth in the grey of a morning, red under grey, waiting. And the boy, who did not have the word, who was the last of Ashford and the first of whatever came after, who had just watched the floor of the world stand up into the sky and had not run, bent his face low over the small warm glow and gave it the only thing the grandmothers had ever had to give a fire in the dark, which was not a prayer and not a name but a promise:

"I'll keep you," he said. "I'll keep you till it's your turn. Hush now."

And above the town the ember-drake stood up red into the morning, and the nine cities of the plain, waking one after another to a light in the west where no sun rises, learned in a single grey hour the thing the founders' law had thrown away and the hearth-tongue had kept safe in the mouths of grandmothers all the long ages between: that the fire does not go out. It is only ever banked. And it was here before the town, and it will be here after, red under the grey, waiting to be woken — and it has been woken now.

---

*So the smell of smoke becomes the light of the flame: the seam under the town draws its last long breath and lets it go as heat, the sealed quarter cracks along the line from well to gate, and the town — leaning, in the end, toward and not away — comes to the lip of the parted stone and faces the red it cannot pray to. The ember-drake comes up not through the gate, where it reached, but through the founders' own well, the door a people dug to see the future and so left fit for the future to come through; it passes the throne it does not take, warms the worn eight at last, and stands up out of the ground into the morning as a column of banked red light, unhurried as a planet, while the nine cities wake to a fire in the west where no sun rises. And under the gate the youngest fire, answered once and forever, glows steady in a boy's two hands — waiting its own turn to come up — kept, as the last keeper of the hearth-tongue promised it would be kept, till the turn is its own. In the ninth chapter the cities must answer what they have seen with their own eyes and can no longer argue against: not a rumor of a god, but the god's elder, standing in the morning; and the league that convened to judge one creature must reckon that there was never only one.*
