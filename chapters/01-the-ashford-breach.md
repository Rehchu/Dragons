# Chapter One — The Ashford Breach

Before it was a god, before it was a verdict, before it was the coiled dark that would sit in the reflecting well of the Hall of Nine Keys and wait to see which of the Nine would understand, it was an egg in the ash — and the ash had been a village once, and the village had been called Ashford, and no one who could still say the name was left to say it.

The boy found it. His name does not matter to the histories and it will not be recorded here, because within the hour he would be one more grey shape in a grey field and there would be no one to carry his name forward; but he was nine years old, and he had come back over the burned ground the way children come back to a wound, to touch the thing that had hurt them and prove to themselves it was real. The fires had burned three days before. What they left was not a landscape but the memory of one — the black beams of houses fallen into the shapes of houses, the wells still standing full of water no one would drink again, the long soft drifts of ash that took a footprint the way new snow does and held it, so that walking Ashford now was walking through a record of every last thing that had tried to run.

In the center of it, where the meeting-hall had been, the ash was deeper and warmer than it had any right to be three days cold. And in the warm heart of that deep ash, half-buried, patient, unhurried as a planet, lay the egg.

## The Egg

It was the size of a curled sleeping man, and it was not shaped like any egg the boy had seen — not the neat ovals of the hens, not the pale globes the river-turtles left in the sand. It was ridged and dark and faintly translucent at its crown, the way a coal is translucent at its heart when you have watched it too long in the dark and your eyes begin to lie to you. It sat in the ash the way a stone sits in a streambed — as though the world had flowed around it for a very long time and worn itself into that exact accommodating shape, and the egg had simply endured, and would go on enduring after the stream and the streambed and the boy and the ash were all of them gone.

He put his hand on it. He was nine; of course he put his hand on it. It was warm — not the dying warmth of the ash around it but a living warmth, a banked and breathing warmth, the warmth of a forge that has been let go grey on top and is all the hotter underneath for the deception. And when his palm settled on the ridged dark shell, the boy felt, running up his arm and into the small cage of his chest, something that was almost a sound and almost a word and was neither: a slow deep note, like the note the great bar of a barred door makes when it drops into its bronze cradle, one toll rolling out through the floor of the world.

He did not run. That was the thing he would not have been able to explain, if there had been anyone left to ask him and any hour left in which to answer. Every animal that could still run had run from Ashford three days ago. The boy did not run. He knelt in the ash beside the warm dark egg, the way — though he could not have known it, the way no one yet living could have known it — the way a marcher lord would one day kneel on cold marble in a sealed hall and say a word that was not in the law. He knelt, and he waited, and he was not afraid of the right thing, because he was too young to know yet that there was a right thing to be afraid of.

The egg's translucent crown began, very faintly, to glow.

## What Came Before

The histories, when they were finally written, could not agree on when the wyrm came to Ashford. Some said dusk of the first day; some swore it had already been among them a full turning of the moon, walking the lanes in some smaller borrowed shape, before it chose to stop pretending. What the histories agreed on — the one splinter of that day they could not argue away, because too many died holding it — was this: that when it came, it came *out of the ground*, out of the deep ash of an older burning that Ashford had built its meeting-hall over and forgotten; and that it came the way weather comes, without malice and without mercy and without the slightest interest in whether it was met with spears.

The spearmen met it with spears. That, too, the histories kept.

There had been a garrison at Ashford — twelve men of the border levy, farmers three seasons of four, soldiers when the beacon-chain lit — and they had done what men do who have been handed a spear and a wall and told the two of them together add up to safety. They had formed their line. They had lowered their points. They had, one of them, a man named in no record but remembered by the shape of what he did, run forward alone when the thing rose steaming out of the meeting-hall floor, and driven his spear into it up to the crossbar, into the dark heat of it, and felt the point go in.

*It bleeds.* That was the discovery that man bought with his life, and it was a true discovery, and it would be carried all the way to the Hall of Nine Keys and set down before the Nine like a coin laid on a table — *it bleeds, what bleeds can be ended.* The wyrm bled. A dark heat ran down the shaft of the spear and over the man's hands, and where it touched him it did not run like blood but caught like oil, and the man was the first of the twelve to burn.

They all burned. The wyrm did not chase them and did not need to. It simply *attended* to them, the way the sea attends to ships, the way a tide attends to a footprint in the sand — without hurry, without hatred, filling the low places, and the low places were the men. When it was done Ashford was a field of grey shapes and standing wells, and the wyrm stood in the middle of its own new silence and did the thing that the survivors — the three who somehow were not there, who had been in the far pasture, who lived only to become unreliable witnesses — could never afterward agree upon and would not, to any bribe or any judge, repeat.

It spoke.

## The Three Words

Three words. On that number the three survivors agreed, though on nothing else. Three words, in a voice that one of them said came from the sky and one said came from under the earth and the third, the youngest, said had come from *inside her own chest*, as though the wyrm had used the drum of her ribs to make the sound and she had merely been present at her own speaking.

The words themselves went to three different graves. One survivor died of the ash-cough within the year and took her version with her. One recanted, said she had heard nothing, no words, only wind, and held to that recantation with the white-knuckled fervor of a person who has decided that a thing must not be true because if it is true then nothing after it can be borne. And the third, the youngest, the one who had felt the words in her own ribs, would say only this, once, to a scribe of the Archive who came asking years later with a marcher lord's letter of introduction: that she would not repeat the three words because she had understood them, and that understanding them was the whole of the harm, and she would not do to another living soul what had been done to her in that field.

"They were not a threat," she told the scribe. "That is what I cannot make anyone believe. They were not a curse and they were not a command. They were an *introduction*." And she folded her hands, which the fire had marked, and said nothing more, and the scribe — whose name was Peryn, and whose own hands the plague-years would one day ruin in their turn — wrote down *introduction* and underlined it twice and understood that he had been given the single most important word in the world and would never be given the three it stood in for.

The wyrm spoke its three words to the ashes of Ashford. And then, having introduced itself to a field of the dead, it did not fly north, where the cold country was and the old high places its kind were said to keep. It did not fly at all, at first. It turned its enormous head, unhurried as a planet, and it looked back the way it had come — down, into the deep warm ash of the meeting-hall, where an egg the size of a curled sleeping man lay glowing faintly at its crown beneath the small still hand of a boy who had not run.

And whatever passed between the great thing standing and the small thing waking — the histories have no word for it, and neither did the boy, who felt the barred-door note roll through him a second time and, this time, felt it answered — whatever passed between them, when it was over the wyrm rose into the smoke of its own making and flew, at last, and it flew not north to the free cold country but *east*, and inland, and *toward* the breach in the Ashford wall where the border levy's beacon still burned; toward the roads that ran from that beacon to the Free Cities; toward, though no living map yet showed the line of it, the Hall of Nine Keys and the dry marble well at its heart and the nine frightened clever animals who would gather there to decide its nature and discover, too late, that its nature had already decided theirs.

It flew *toward* the cage. Every account keeps that. It flew toward the thing that could hold it, into the arms of the only people organized enough to build a chain strong enough to bind a god — and it went, the way a letter goes toward the hand it was addressed to, the way a word goes toward the one ear that will understand it and be ruined by the understanding.

## The Ash Settles

By the time the cities' outriders reached Ashford — days later, drawn by the beacon and by the terrible quiet after it, riding under the grey-and-iron banners of a magistracy that had outlived every threat it was written to answer and had never once answered one that breathed — there was nothing left in the burned village to bury and no one left to bury it. They counted the standing wells. They walked the drifts of ash and read, in the footprints held there like a record, every last direction the dying had chosen to run. They found the twelve grey shapes of the garrison line and one more, smaller, grey and curled in the deep warm heart of the meeting-hall ash, one small hand still open as though it had lately held something round.

They did not find the egg. The ash where it had lain was cooler now than the ash around it — a clean cold hollow the exact size and shape of a curled sleeping thing, swept smooth, as though whatever had rested there had risen carefully so as not to wake the field, and gone on ahead of them, east and inland, toward the cities and the cage and the well.

One of the outriders, a young one, knelt at the edge of that clean cold hollow and could not have said why the sight of an empty place in the ash should frighten him more than all the grey shapes of the dead. He was afraid, and he did not yet know it, of the right thing. He rose, and mounted, and rode east after his fellows to carry word to the Free Cities that a wyrm had come out of the old burning under Ashford and burned it new; that it bled, and could therefore — surely, surely — be ended; and that, unaccountably, against every instinct of every beast that had ever lived, it had not fled its hunters but ridden the smoke straight down the throat of their roads, as though it had somewhere it very much wished to be.

He did not carry word of the egg, because he had not found it. No one had. It was already ahead of them all, warm and patient and unhurried as a planet, moving east toward the Hall where the founders had once read omens in still water — one thing gone into the arms of the cities to be judged, and one thing following after in the ash it left, unwritten in any account, waiting for a chapter no council would ever convene to hear.

*So begins the saga. In the next chapter, the Free Cities learn what has come down their roads to meet them, and set about the terrible ordinary work of building a cage — measuring the chain, forging the links, choosing the men — never once asking why a thing that could unmake iron with a breath should hold so still to let iron be closed around it; while behind the marching column, in the churned mud of the road east, a small warm hollow keeps appearing in the ash of every fire the outriders leave behind, the exact size and shape of a curled sleeping thing, always one night's travel back, always coming on.*
