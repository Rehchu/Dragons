# Chapter Two — The Taking of the Wyrm

It is a strange labor, to build a cage for a thing that has flown of its own will into your country, and stranger still to build it under the eye of the thing itself, and strangest of all to feel — as every smith and every warden and every levied farmer on that road came in time to feel — that the thing was not so much submitting to the cage as *inspecting the workmanship*. The Free Cities set about the making of the chain the way they set about everything: soberly, in committee, with a ledger. What they did not set down in the ledger, because there was no column for it, was the growing and unspeakable suspicion of every man who lifted a hammer in those weeks — that he was forging his half of a bargain whose other half had been agreed to long before he was born.

The word had come east ahead of the wyrm, as the outrider carried it: that a creature had risen out of the old burning under Ashford and burned it new, that it bled and could therefore be ended, and that — against every instinct of every beast that had ever lived — it had not fled its hunters but come down the throat of their roads toward them, unhurried, as though it had an appointment. The councils of the nine magistracies read the word, and disbelieved it, and then the second rider came, and the third, each with the same account and none able to improve on it, and disbelief became the more terrible thing that comes after disbelief, which is *policy*.

## The Commission of the Chain

They convened a lesser council for it — not the full Nine, not yet; the Hall of Nine Keys was a thing you opened only when the world had left you no smaller door, and the cities were not yet ready to admit the world had done that. What they opened instead was the Foundry Commission, a working body of guild-masters and quartermasters and one warden to sign the warrants, and they gave it a charge that no commission in the history of the league had ever been given: to build a fetter for a thing they had not measured, out of a metal they were not sure would hold it, against a strength they knew only by its work in the grey field of Ashford.

The guild-masters did what guild-masters do. They argued. The Ironmongers wanted a chain of graduated links, each the size of a plough-yoke, drop-forged and quenched and proof-tested to a load no team of oxen could name. The Wallsmen's engineers wanted no chain at all but a pit — a shaft sunk into the bedrock beneath the road, walled in dressed stone, a thing you did not *hold* the creature in so much as *drop the world on top of it*. The Archive, when it was consulted, sent a single dry note that said the surest cage was the one the creature had chosen, and that the commission would be wiser to study why it came than to strain over how to keep it; and the commission, who had a warrant to fulfill and a fear to feed and no idea at all how to act on that note, filed it, and forged the chain.

It took nine days and the better part of three cities' charcoal. When it was done it was the largest single work of iron the Free Cities had ever made — links like the ribs of a beached whale, black and oil-sheened and cooling in the yard, a length of it that four teams could barely drag, and at its head a collar hinged and pinned and sized by a guess against the neck of a thing no one on the commission had dared approach close enough to measure. They looked at it, the whole exhausted commission, in the red light of the banked furnaces, and no one said the thing they were all thinking, which was that it was beautiful, and that it was enormous, and that it would not be enough — that whatever this was for, the iron was not the important part of it, and had never been.

## The Column

They rode out to meet the wyrm because the wyrm would not be hurried, and a thing that will not be hurried must be *escorted*, or the escort is left standing in the road holding a warrant and a chain and looking a fool. The column that went west from the cities was the largest muster since the last lighting of the beacon-chain: two hundred of the border levy under their captains, the whole strength of the Wallsmen's road-guard, the outriders who had first carried the word and knew the ground, the wains that dragged the chain, the smiths who would set it, and — riding at the head under the grey-and-iron banners, because someone of rank had to be seen to command a thing no one could command — a third-chair councillor of the Southern Marches, sent because his was the seat nearest the Ashford road and because the fuller Nine had not yet steeled themselves to come.

His name was Aldric ban Sorel, and this was the first time he saw it.

He would kneel to it, in time, on cold marble in a sealed hall, and name it a word that was not in the law. But that was still weeks and a whole understanding away. On the Ashford road he was only a marcher lord doing a marcher lord's work, riding at the head of a column sent to fetch a monster, and when the column crested the low rise east of the ruined village and he saw the thing for the first time — coiled in the road's own dust in the long gold light of evening, vast and dark and steaming faintly at the flanks where the day's heat left it, its furnace eyes already open and already, unmistakably, turned toward the rise where he stood — Aldric felt the first cold finger of the thing that would one day put him on his knees. He did not name it. He did not have the word yet. He only knew, the way you know weather is turning, that they had brought two hundred spears and a whale's-weight of iron to a place where none of it was the point.

The wyrm watched the column come. It did not rise. It did not flee, and it did not attack, and it did not — this was the part the levy-men would speak of for the rest of their lives, in low voices, when they had drunk enough to speak of it at all — it did not seem to *notice* the spears at all, the way a mountain does not notice the goats on its shoulder. It lay in the road it had chosen and it waited for them to come to it, and in the waiting it made the whole armed strength of the Free Cities feel like what it was: a great many frightened clever animals, arrived, with tools.

## The Setting of the Collar

Someone had to be first to approach it. The captains drew for it, or said they did; the outriders muttered that the draw was arranged, that the youngest was sent because the young are cheapest, and the histories, when they came to be written, could not settle it and did not try. What is kept is this: that a smith of the Ironmongers' guild, a broad quiet man who had helped forge the collar and therefore felt, in some crooked way, responsible for it, walked out alone across the last hundred paces of dust with the hinged iron in his arms and the whole column at his back holding its breath, and that the wyrm let him come.

It let him come. That was the taking, entire — not a battle, not a snaring, not the brave and bloody thing the ballads would afterward insist it must have been. The wyrm lowered its enormous head, unhurried as a planet, down to the dust, down to the level of a standing man, and it held it there, still as the marble it would one day lie against, and it let the smith close the collar around a throat that the iron barely spanned, and it let him drive the pin, and it did not so much as ripple when the pin went home. The heat came off it in waves that curled the hair on the smith's arms and dried the sweat before it fell. The eyes, that close, filled the whole world, furnace-red through smoked glass, and the smith looked into them because he could not help it, and whatever he saw there he carried to his grave and gave to no one, and was never afterward, his wife said, entirely warm again.

The collar closed. The chain paid out, link by ringing link, back across the hundred paces to the wains. Two hundred spearmen let out a breath they had held so long that the sound of it moved through the column like wind through a wheatfield. And the wyrm — bound now, collared now, chained to a whale's-weight of the best iron the Free Cities could forge, held at last by the only people organized enough to hold it — lifted its head again, slow as a tide, and looked back down the length of the chain at the men who thought they had done something.

Aldric ban Sorel, watching from the rise, understood for one lurching moment what Peryn of the Archive would say aloud in a sealed hall weeks later, though he had not yet the words to think it in: *one does not surrender to be judged. One surrenders to be recognized.* The chain was not on the wyrm. The chain was on the cities. They had simply not felt it close.

## The Long Road East

They turned the column and they brought it home, and the bringing took eleven days, and every one of the eleven was worse than the burning would have been, because a burning ends and this did not.

The wyrm walked. That was the mercy and the horror of it — it *walked*, at the pace of the ox-wains, on limbs that folded and unfolded with the awful patience of something with all the time there had ever been, and it never once strained the chain, never tested the collar, never gave the column the clean terror of a thing fighting to be free. It came where they led it because it wished to go where they were going, and the not-fighting was worse than fighting, because a thing that fights can be beaten and a thing that comes willingly cannot be anything but *arriving*.

The road-guard rationed the wyrm's silence between them like a watch. In the days it walked and they walked and the country came out to the roadside to see the monster the cities had caught, and went home quieter than it had come, and shut its doors. In the nights they made their fires and posted their watches and did not sleep, and the wyrm lay down at the end of its chain, coiled, banked, radiating a forge-heat that the near sentries came in time to be grateful for on the cold nights and ashamed of their gratitude in the mornings. It did not eat. No one saw it eat, in eleven days, and this too went unspoken, folded into the general dread, one more column with no place in the ledger.

And behind them — though not one man of two hundred marked it, because it was always exactly where a man does not look, one night's travel back, in the churned mud where the column had already passed — a small warm hollow kept appearing in the ash of every fire the column left cold behind it: swept smooth, unhurried, the exact size and shape of a curled sleeping thing, coming on. The rear-guard rode over the same ground the next dawn a dozen times in eleven days and found, a dozen times, a place in last night's ashes warmer than it had any right to be, and each time told himself it was a trick of the wind, or a beast come to the warmth, or nothing; and each time was afraid, and did not know he was afraid of the right thing.

## The Gates of the City

On the eleventh day they came within sight of the walls, and the cities did the last of the ordinary work: they decided where to put a god.

They did not call it that, of course. There was no motion before any council to call it that; the word was not in the law and would not be for weeks yet, and when it came it would come from the mouth of the very marcher lord now riding drawn and silent at the head of the column, and it would come in a sealed hall from which no one would afterward walk out unchanged. On the eleventh day it was still only *the creature*, *the matter taken at the Ashford breach*, and the question before the quartermasters was merely logistical: the Foundry Commission's stone pit was not finished, the walled shaft in the bedrock stood half-dug and would stand so for months, and a thing already chained cannot be left standing in the road while the masons catch up.

So they did the reasonable thing, the practical thing, the thing that a magistracy of ledgers and warrants does when the extraordinary arrives ahead of the accommodations built for it. They looked at the map of the city for a space already strong enough, already deep enough, already built — as the old note from the Archive had dryly promised — *to hold judgment*. And they found one. There was a hall at the city's heart that had not been opened to full council in forty years, whose bronze doors three grown wardens strained to move, at whose center stood a dry marble well where the founders had once read the future in a pool of water. It was the strongest room the Free Cities had ever built. It had been built, the founders' own inscription said, *to hold judgment*.

They led the wyrm to the Hall of Nine Keys because it was the nearest cage strong enough, and the wardens hauled the great bronze doors apart, and the doors screamed — a long metal complaint that ran up the vaulted throat of the hall and died in the dark above the lamps — and the creature that had walked eleven days at the pace of an ox-wain went in through those screaming doors without a check in its stride, down the aisle, to the dry well at the heart of the room, and folded itself into the marble basin like smoke pouring backward into a bottle, and lay still, and waited.

It had come, in the end, exactly where it meant to come. The cities had built the cage; the cities had forged the chain; the cities had chosen, out of all the rooms in all their nine magistracies, the one room built to hold judgment, and had led the thing into it with their own hands and their own best iron, and had thought, the whole eleven days, that they were the ones doing the leading.

Aldric ban Sorel stood in the screaming doorway and looked down the length of the hall at the coiled dark settling into the well where omens had once been read, and he felt the appointment he had felt on the rise east of Ashford close over him like water — the certainty that the important thing had not happened yet, that none of the iron and none of the spears and none of the eleven careful days had been the point, that the point was still coming, and that when it came it would come for him. He did not yet know he would kneel. He did not yet have the word. But he stood in the doorway of the Hall of Nine Keys a little longer than command required, and he was the last of the column to look away.

## The Ash at the Threshold

That night, when the wyrm was sealed in and the exhausted column stood down and the city's first uneasy rumor ran wall to wall that the monster was *inside*, was *within the walls*, was in the old hall at the very heart of them — that night a boy of the night-watch, walking his round of the outer gate through which the column had passed at dusk, found in the cold ash of a sentry-fire a hollow that had no business being there. It was swept smooth. It was the size and shape of a curled sleeping thing. And when he crouched and, foolish, young, put out his hand, it was warm — not the dying warmth of the ash around it but a living warmth, a banked and breathing warmth, and rising up his arm and into the small cage of his chest came something that was almost a sound and almost a word and was neither: a slow deep note, like the note a great bar makes when it drops into its bronze cradle, one toll rolling out through the floor of the world.

He did not run. He could never afterward say why. He knelt in the cold ash beside the warm hollow, at the threshold of the city that had just taken one wyrm into the strongest room it owned, and he did not know — no one yet living could have known — that the taking was only half done; that one thing had gone into the arms of the cities to be judged, and one thing had followed it home in the ash, unwritten in any ledger, unchained by any collar, and now lay warm and patient and unhurried as a planet at the very gate, waiting for a chapter no council would ever convene to hear.

---

*So closes the taking. In the third chapter the fuller Nine at last unbar the door they have avoided for forty years and convene in the sealed hall to render their verdict upon the thing they have chained — never once asking why a creature that could unmake iron with a breath walked eleven days in their collar to sit, of its own will, in the one room they built to hold judgment; while at the gate behind them, in the cold ash, a small warm hollow keeps its own counsel and its own patience, and waits.*
