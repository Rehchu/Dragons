# Chapter Nine — The Nine Cities

A thing that happens to one town is a story, and a thing that happens to nine at once is a history, and the difference between them is not size but *witness* — for a story can be told by the one who lived it, and a history can only be assembled afterward out of nine tellings that do not agree, by someone who was not there. There was no such someone, that morning. The one man in all the plain who might have written it true was sealed in a temple with his knees on the stone and the word still coming out of him; and so what the nine cities did in the grey hour when the light stood up in the west has come down to us the way histories come down, in pieces, each city sure it woke first, each sure it saw most, none of them knowing that the same instant had found all nine at once and struck them like the nine keys of one lock turned by one hand.

For that is the thing to understand before anything else: it was not nine wakings. It was one note, and nine hearings of it.

## The Note That Reached Them All

The column of banked red light went up out of the parted ground over the gate-town in silence, and silence, in a fire that size, is not the absence of sound but a sound too large to be heard as one — the way the sea is not silent and yet a man standing beside it hears no single thing, only the whole. What went out from the ember-drake's standing was not a roar the beacons could relay nor a tremor the eastern baronies could log in their earth-shudder ledgers. It was the great struck-bell note — the same tone the egg had made in the ash, the same the barred doors of the Hall had made in the sealed hour, the same that had gathered, note by note over the long chapters, into a chord — and it went out now not up the beacon-chain and not along the post-roads but *through the ground the cities were standing on*, because the ground the cities were standing on was the thing that had made the note, and there is no faster road to a city than the floor of it.

So the note reached them all in the same instant, and the same instant is the closest thing to *together* that nine quarrelsome cities had known since the Long Cold, and not one of them recognized it, because a people that has agreed on nothing for forty years does not know the feeling of agreeing on one thing at once, and mistakes it, every time, for the end of the world. Which, to be fair to them, it also was.

The note reached them, and each city heard it in the tongue of the thing it most trusted, for that is how a great note is heard — not as itself, but as the trembling of whatever in you was already tuned to tremble.

## Nine Hearings

**In the River Guilds**, where Vessa's chair had stood empty eleven days and her deputy had spent those days pretending the emptiness was ordinary, the note came up through the wet stone of the wharves and set the moored barges knocking their hulls together all along the quay, one after another down the whole curved mile of the water, *knock, knock, knock,* the river itself passing the note hull to hull the way the plain had passed the rumor ear to ear; and the deputy of the River Guilds, a careful woman named Harl who had voted nothing in her life and been proud of it, stood on the wharf and felt the boards lean under her toward the western light and understood, with the particular cold of a careful person, that her councillor was not coming back, and that the emptiness she had been pretending was ordinary had never been Vessa's chair being briefly vacant but Vessa's chair being *finished*, and that she, Harl, who had wanted only to hold a place for someone, was now the place itself.

**In the Wallsmen's city**, Hedric's second was a wall-captain named Doon who had waited his whole career for the day the beacons would light in earnest and the levies would matter and a hard man would finally be *necessary again* — which was Hedric's own hunger, worn down the line to his deputy the way a coin's face wears down through pockets — and the day had come, the beacons were lit, the western sky was a standing fire, and Doon climbed to the highest tower of the wall his city was named for to give the order he had rehearsed for thirty years, and opened his mouth, and gave no order, because there was nothing on the horizon to war upon. A wall is a line you hold against a thing that comes *at* you. The fire in the west did not come at anyone. It stood. And a hard man on a high wall, looking at a thing that will not come at him and cannot be gone out to, discovers the one enemy his whole trade never taught him to fight, which is a thing that simply *is*, and does not care that he is armed; and Doon put his hands on the parapet his city had built to outlast every threat it was written to answer, and leaned — toward the fire, not away — and his hands, which knew the wall's stone better than his own face, felt for the first time that the wall was thin.

**In the Coinhouse**, where Tobane the Younger had inherited his seat and his fear and taken both into the temple and left neither behind him, the deputy did the only thing the Coinhouse had ever known how to do with a new fact, which was to price it. He sat at the long counting-board in the grey before dawn with the western light already reddening the wax of his tally-candles and he set his beads to reckon the cost of a fire in the west — the barges that would not sail, the levies that would eat and not earn, the debt in men the eastern baronies would now surely default, the whole ledger of a plain that had woken to a god — and as he reached to move the first bead, the beads moved themselves. Not much. Once. The whole nine rows of them slid a finger's width along their wires, all together, toward the west, toward the light, the way iron filings turn to a lodestone, the way the barges knocked, the way the boards leaned, the way a field of grain faces a sun; and the deputy of the Coinhouse looked at his abacus leaning west and understood that there was now a thing in the world that even money faced, that even money could not price because it stood on the far side of the last column, where the sums run out; and he did not finish the reckoning, and no one ever finished it, and that unfinished sum is the truest account the Coinhouse ever kept.

**In the Kilnward**, the city of the fire-guilds, of the potters and the glaziers and the kiln-masters, Ormund's seat had been the first of the sealed eight to buckle — a master of fire kneeling to the master of fire — and his city felt the note in its kilns. Every banked kiln in the Kilnward, the great slow ovens that are never fully let go out because relighting them costs a day, drew its draught at once, all together, toward the west, the way the seam under the gate-town had drawn its breath before it spoke; and the kiln-masters who had tended those fires all their lives, who could read a flame's health by its color the way a physician reads a face, saw their own hearths lean and pull toward a fire greater than any hearth and knew, in the marrow of a trade, the thing the law had spent forty years forgetting: that all their kept fires were the children of one fire, that they had been tending the small warm grandchildren of the thing now standing in the west, and had called it *craft*, and taken wages for it. The Kilnward did not run. The Kilnward, of all the nine, understood soonest — not with its councillors, who were sealed, but with its hands, which were not.

**In the Fenreeve**, the low wet country south of the river where Lisenne kept the dikes, the water heard the note before the people did. The fen is a country that has made its peace with the truth every other city spends itself denying — that water always finds the low place, that the holding-back is temporary and the flood is the rule — and so when the note came up through the standing water of the fen it did not knock or lean or draw a draught; it simply *rose*, a hand's breadth, all across the fen at once, the black water lifting to the note the way the gate-stones had lifted to the breathing under them, and settled, and rose again, and the fen-folk who woke to water at their thresholds where no rain had fallen did not cry flood, because a fen-folk knows the difference between a flood and a tide, and this was a tide — the tide of a thing pulling the whole low country gently toward itself, unhurried, patient, certain, the way the fen had always known the sea would come for it in the end and had built its whole slow culture around outlasting the certainty of losing. Lisenne's deputy, standing to her ankles in risen black water in the grey, said the thing the fen says, that Lisenne herself would have said and could not, being sealed and weeping and warm at last in the temple: *water always finds the low place.* And then, looking west at the standing fire the risen water leaned toward, she said the second half of it, the half the fen does not usually say aloud: *and we are the low place.*

**In the Southern Marches**, Aldric's own holding, the marcher city nearest the Ashford road and so nearest the whole beginning of it, the note came up through a land already half-emptied — for the Marches had sent their third-chair west with a column eleven days ago and he had not come back, and a border holding whose lord rides out and does not return learns fast to run itself on rumor, and rumor was all the Marches had. They had heard, thirdhand and denied, that their lord had knelt. They had heard he had named the creature a god. They had heard, and disbelieved, and heard again, until the disbelieving was worn as thin as the deputies' lie, and then the fire stood up in the west, plainly, undeniably, in the exact direction their lord had ridden and not come back from, and the Marches understood in one grey hour the thing the whole rest of the plain would take longer to understand: that Aldric had not gone mad in the sealed hall. Aldric had gone *first*. He had seen, before any of them, the thing now standing in the sky where anyone could see it, and had done the only honest thing a man can do who sees it, which is kneel; and the Marches, who had been ashamed of their kneeling lord for eleven days, stopped being ashamed, and were the first city of the nine to say the word out loud in daylight and mean it — not as rumor, not as the thing the deputies denied, but as the plain description of the fire in their own western sky.

**In the Archive**, where Peryn's seat was, the note came up through the stacks, and the stacks answered it in the one way stacks can, which is that things fell. Down the long cold aisles where the Free Cities kept everything they had ever written down and most of what they had agreed to forget, the note ran through the shelving and the old bindings shifted and slid and here and there a volume tipped and fell open in the dark, and the under-archivists who came running with lamps found, in the grey, that the books which had fallen were not random — or were random, and it was the Archive's particular curse to be unable to believe anything was random — but among the fallen, face-up on the stone floor in the lamplight, was a book none of them could read, in the old orthography, from before the new law, one of the ones Peryn had meant when he said, in the sealed hall, the truest thing said in the whole saga: *we kept the shelf, and lost the book.* They had kept it. It had been on the shelf the whole time, the book with the lost word in it, the word the law had thrown away because it could not be legislated — kept, unread, in the dark, the way the hearth-tongue had kept the little word in the mouths of grandmothers, the two ends of the same forgetting; and the note that stood the ember-drake up in the west shook it off its shelf at last and laid it open on the floor where no one alive could read it, which is the Archive's whole tragedy in one image: they had saved the word and lost the reading of it, and now the thing the word named was standing in the sky, and the book that could have told them what to call it lay open and mute in the lamplight, keeping its secret perfectly, exactly as it had been built to do.

And **the two who were not seats of cities** heard it too. Callenreth, the High Warden, had no city — the High Warden presides over all nine and belongs to none, which had felt, for forty years, like wisdom, and felt now, sealed in the temple with his presiding worn out of him, like the loneliest fate the law could have designed: to be the one seat with no wall to lean on, no kiln to draw, no water to rise, no deputy standing in for him in any city because he *was* all the cities and therefore none. And Sera Lowe, the youngest, the only one of the Nine born after the Long Cold, had never fit a city either — she had wanted the creature *studied*, cages and calipers and lamplight, and her city, if she had one, was the future the study was meant to serve. Neither of them woke to the note in a city. They woke in the temple, worn smooth or nearly, and the note that stood their elder up in the west did not reach them through wall or water or kiln but through the floor of the sealed hall directly, unmediated, the raw thing itself with no city's trust to translate it; and that, perhaps, is why the two who heard it purest are the two the histories remember least, for a note heard with nothing between you and it cannot be told afterward — it can only be *survived*, or not.

## The Map Draws Itself

Here is a thing the nine cities had never done, in all the forty years of their league, and did that morning without meaning to: they thought of themselves as nine.

It sounds like nothing. But a league is a legal fiction held together by debt and by the memory of a shared cold, and the citizens of it do not, day to day, feel themselves to be part of a nine — they feel themselves to be of the Kilnward, or the Fenreeve, or the River Guilds, and the other eight are the places their debts are owed and their grievances kept. The map of the league had always been a map of *edges* — this city's levy owed to that one, this warden's water-right disputed with that fen — a map you read the way you read a ledger, by the lines between the columns. The fire in the west redrew it. For the first time, standing at their thresholds in the grey and leaning all the same direction, the nine cities were not nine columns in a ledger with lines of debt between them; they were nine points on the rim of one circle, and the fire was the center, and a circle is a shape you cannot read as a ledger, because it has no columns and no edges, only a middle that every point on it faces.

The map drew itself, that morning, the way the seam had drawn itself on the surface of the gate-town — not by any hand, but by the thing underneath rising into view. And on the map that drew itself, the nine cities saw, all at once and for the first time, the shape they had always truly been: not a league of nine against the cold, but nine fires kept small on the back of one fire kept sleeping, nine kilns tended by the grandchildren of the flame they now faced, a ring of little warmths that had spent forty years disputing the debts between them and never once looked *down*, at the thing they were all, every one, standing on.

## There Was Never Only One

And then, having looked down, they looked up, and understood the last thing, which is the thing this chapter was written to carry and the thing the whole saga had been walking toward since a wyrm rose out of an older burning under a town called Ashford: that the fire in the west was not the creature they had chained.

They had chained one. They had spent nine days forging a chain for one, and eleven days walking one home, and a sealed council judging one, and a whole plain arguing over whether the one was a beast or an enemy or a god. One. The rumor was of one. The deputies' lie was about one. Every quarrel of the whole long turning had been a quarrel about a single creature, singular, chained, held — and now a second stood in the west, elder and enormous and never chained at all, come up out of the ground the cities were built on to warm the first; and if there were two, then the number was not one, and if the number was not one, then it had never been one, then the *one* had only ever been the one they happened to have caught, the way a single fish in a net is not the number of the fish in the sea but only the number of the fish in the net; and the whole architecture of the league's forty-year peace, which had been built on the comfortable arithmetic of threats you could count — one cold, one war, one creature, one chain — cracked along that line the way the sealed quarter had cracked from well to gate, all at once, silently, on the surface of every mind in the nine cities at the same grey instant.

There was never only one.

It is a small sentence. It undoes everything. For a thing you can count, you can cage — you forge a chain the size of it, you dig a pit the depth of it, you convene a council the gravity of it, and when it is caught you are safe, because the one is caught. But a thing you cannot count you cannot cage, because there is no size to forge the chain to; and the cities had built their whole civilization — their kilns, their walls, their coin, their archive, their law that threw away the word — on the unspoken faith that the dangerous things of the world were things that could, in the end, be counted and so contained. The fire in the west was the end of that faith. Not because it was large. Because it was *another*, and another means *how many more*, and *how many more* is the question a chain cannot answer, and never could, and the cities had simply never been made to ask it until a second fire stood up in a morning sky and asked it for them.

Under the gate, where the whole thing had its smallest and truest center, the boy who kept the youngest fire felt the great column's note where it hung over the town, and felt, in his two hands, the egg answer it — not lost now, not calling into the empty dark, but striking its own note clean and sure into a world it now knew was full of its kind; and the boy, who had no city and no seat and no vote and knew nothing of leagues or ledgers or the arithmetic of chains, understood the chapter's last truth before any councillor or deputy or High Warden did, because he had known it the longest and by the surest organ, which was his hands: that the warm thing he kept was not the last of anything, nor the first, but one of *many*, one note in a chord that ran down under the whole plain and back before the towns and forward past them; that the fire in the west had a brother in the ash under his fingers, and that brother had cousins yet unwoken banked in the ground under all nine cities, under the wall and the wharf and the counting-board and the fen and the fallen book, waiting, as this one waited, for a cold hand and a grey morning and someone who would not run.

He was not afraid of the number. He was the only one on the whole plain that morning who was not afraid of the number, because he was the only one who had already been keeping one, and you cannot be afraid of *how many more* when you have knelt in the ash by one of them and promised to keep it till its turn. The cities feared the number because to them each new fire was one more thing that could not be caught. The boy did not fear it, because to him each new fire was one more thing that could be *kept*.

That is the whole difference between the cities and the boy, and it is the difference the rest of the saga is the argument of.

---

*So the light stands up in the west and the nine cities hear one note in nine tongues — the barges knock it down the wharf, the wall leans thin, the abacus-beads slide west of the last column, the banked kilns draw their draught, the fen-water rises to it, the Marches say their kneeling lord's word at last in daylight, and in the Archive the lost book falls open, mute, on the stone. The league that had always read itself as a ledger of edges is redrawn in one grey hour into a ring of nine little warmths facing a fire at its center, and looks down for the first time at the sleeping thing it built itself upon — and then looks up, and understands the sentence that undoes the arithmetic of every chain it ever forged: there was never only one. Under the gate the youngest fire strikes its note clean into a world it knows now is full of its kind, and the boy who keeps it is the one soul on all the plain unafraid of the number, because he does not count the fires — he keeps them. Now the cities must decide, each in its own dread, what a people does when the thing it feared cannot be counted: muster against it, as the eastern debt will demand; or remember, as the Long Cold has been waiting all this while to be remembered.*
