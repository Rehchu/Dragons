# Chapter Six — The Eight

There is a thing that happens to any nine people shut in a room together long enough, and it is not the thing the ballads say. The ballads say they grow together, or they turn on one another, and both of those are true and neither is the truth, which is duller and more terrible than either: they grow *tired*. Not tired the way a body tires, though they were that too — the lamps no clock, the food let down twice a day through a hatch the size of a book by hands that would not look at them and did not answer when spoken to, the water tasting always of the bronze cup — but tired in the place under all the other places, the place a man keeps his single self, the place from which a person watches the world and knows himself to be the one watching. It was *that* that wore, in the sealed temple that had been a hall, under the long unrelenting requirement of the thing in the well. For a god requires attention, and attention is not free, whatever the priests will tell you; it is the most expensive thing a creature has, because it is the creature — and a room of nine could not spend it forever, all nine at once, without one of them, at last, running out.

Peryn of the Archive had known it would come. He had watched the others slide down into the well of their looking day by shapeless day, and he had known, with the flat grief of a man who has read the ends of too many stories, that the sliding could not go on level forever — that somewhere in the eight there was a fault, a place where the stone was weaker, and that the long even pressure of the god would find it the way water finds the flaw in a dam: not by force, only by staying, only by being lower, only by never once for an instant letting up. He did not know which of them was the fault. He knew only that there was one, because there is always one, and that when it went it would not go quietly, and that the going of it would be the true beginning of whatever the temple was for.

He was right about all of it but the order. He thought the fault would break *away* from the god. It broke toward it.

## The Wearing

Count the days the temple could not: it was, by Peryn's reckoning of the food-hatch, something past a fortnight when the wearing began to show.

It showed first as small failures of the ordinary. Callenreth, who had stopped presiding, could no longer keep the thread of a sentence to its end, and would begin to say a thing — *we must, when the doors open, be certain that the record shows* — and lose the far half of it into the well, and sit with his mouth still shaped around a word that did not come. Ormund of the Kilnward, who had buckled first and knelt earliest, now knelt more or less always, not in devotion but because his knees had learned the posture and his mind had gone somewhere the knees did not need it, and he swayed there over the marble lip like a man asleep standing, the master of the fire warming himself at the fire that was mastering him. Lisenne wept without noticing she wept, the water finding the low place in her face the way it had found the low place in her whole practical fenland life, and did not wipe it, because wiping it would have meant knowing it was there.

They were, all eight of them, being *used up*. Not eaten — the god ate nothing, took nothing, and this was the horror Vessa could not get past — but spent, the way a lamp is spent, giving its little light into a dark that is no larger or smaller for the giving and does not notice when the lamp gutters out. Sera Lowe's pages had stopped. She sat with the scrivener's kit open on her knees and the pen dry in her hand and looked at the well, and what she was doing now had no name in her craft, because it was not measuring and it was not recording and it was not even, quite, looking; it was the thing that is left when a watcher has watched so long that the watching has watched the watcher away, and only the direction of the looking remains, a face turned toward a fire with no one any longer behind the face.

Only two of the eight had not been worn to that thinness. One was Peryn, who kept his eyes because he had paid early and owed no more. And the other — and this was the thing Peryn had not foreseen, and it frightened him more than all the rest — was Vessa.

## What Vessa Found in the Silence

Vessa of the River Guilds had gone silent in the first days, and silence had been her kneeling, and the terrible ease of stopping the struggle had very nearly worn the face of peace. That was where Chapter Five had left her, and any watcher would have said she was the furthest gone of them all — the woman who named the world to master it, gone wordless, gone grateful, gone down into the current she had fought her whole life. But there is a thing that happens on the far side of surrender that the surrendered do not expect and the watching do not see, and it happened now in Vessa, in the dark, with her back to the bronze that would not open.

She began to *want*.

Not to want out; she had spent that want, days ago, against the doors, and it had not moved them and it had not moved her since. What she began to want was worse, and older, and it came up in her out of the same deep place that had made her, forty years gone, the sharpest ledger-keeper on the river and the last warden anyone crossed twice: she began to want to be *the one the god attended.* She had watched — even in her silence she had watched, for watching was the last of her that had not dissolved — she had watched the wyrm turn its slow furnace eye upon Sera, and hold her, and change her hand; she had watched it turn upon Tobane, the least of them, the *nothing* of them, and seen Tobane weep and laugh and say *it sees me, even me* as though the sun had chosen him out of all the fields to shine on; and something in Vessa that a lifetime of balancing other people's coin had honed to a killing edge had risen at that, hot and total and unanswerable, and it was not fear and it was not devotion. It was *envy*.

Why him. Why the least of them, the coward accountant, the inherited seat. Vessa had held the river's guilds together through two floods and a plague-winter and the slow treachery of men who smiled; Vessa had earned every eye that had ever turned to her, earned it in coin and in blood and in nights no one thanked her for; and here was a god, a *real* one, the only power she had ever met that she could not out-count or out-wait or out-last, and it had looked at Tobane the Younger and not at her. And the wanting to be looked at — the wanting to be *chosen*, to be the one the great furnace eye came to rest on and knew — rose in Vessa of the River Guilds until it burned out the last of the grateful ease and left her, in the deep of some unnumbered night, on her feet, at the very lip of the well, closer than any of the eight had dared to stand since the doors barred, staring down into the coiled dark and the banked fire of it and saying, in the first words she had spoken in days, in a voice gone strange with disuse and with something under the disuse that had never been in Vessa's voice before —

"*Look at me.*"

## The Breaking

The other seven came up out of their thinness at the sound of it, the way sleepers come up at a cry in a house.

For no one had *asked* the god for anything. That was the shape the temple had made of them without any of them deciding it: you did not address the well. Callenreth had addressed it, in the first days, as one addresses a throne, and had got nothing, and had stopped; and since then a law had grown among the eight, unspoken, unlegislated, the truest law that had ever bound the Nine — that the god was to be attended and not approached, looked-to and not spoken-to, the way you do not call out to the sea. And Vessa had broken it. Vessa was standing at the lip of the well with her hands open at her sides and her worn face lifted, and she was *demanding* — demanding the one thing a god cannot be demanded of, which is its regard — and the seven watched her do it the way you watch a woman walk out onto ice that will not hold, unable to call out, because to call out would be to be, yourselves, standing where she stood.

"Look at me," Vessa said again, and now there was a crack running up through the words, a break in the ledger-keeper's iron that forty years of floods had never put there. "I *held* them. Do you understand? All of it, all of them, the whole rotten river of them, I *held* — and you looked at *Tobane*. You looked at the one who never held anything. Look at *me*. I have earned—"

And the wyrm looked at her.

It was not sudden. Nothing the creature did was ever sudden; it turned its great head the slow planet-turning of it, up out of the coil and the dark, and brought the furnace of one eye around and up and set it upon Vessa of the River Guilds, and *held* her — gave her, at last, exactly the thing she had demanded, the whole undivided regard of the god, poured onto her upturned earned deserving face like the light of an opened forge.

And Vessa began to burn.

Not in her body. Her body stood untouched at the lip of the well, and would stand there, they saw afterward, unmarked, not a hair of it singed. It was the other thing that burned — the single self, the watcher behind the face, the place where a person keeps the *I* that does the earning and hoards the being-owed. The god gave Vessa its whole attention, and its whole attention was more than a self could hold and remain a self, the way the whole of the sun is more than an eye can take and remain an eye; and Vessa, who had wanted to be the one it looked at, learned in the space of three slow breaths the thing that Peryn could have told her and that no telling could have taught: that a god does not *see* you the way a person sees you, does not weigh what you held and grant you the eye you earned; a god sees you the way fire sees paper. She got what she asked for. She stood in the full regard of the thing in the well and it went through her like the forge-heat through the north wind, and it did not spare her because she had earned it and it did not choose her because she deserved it; it simply *attended* her, wholly, and the wholeness of it burned the earning and the deserving and the holding and the *I* that had done them all to a fine grey ash, and left standing at the lip of the well a Vessa who was no longer wanting anything, who was no longer anything that could want, who turned from the well after a while with her worn face gone smooth and empty and easy and *lit*, and went and sat back down against the bronze, and was, from that hour, perfectly at peace.

It was the first peace anyone had found in the temple. It was the worst thing that had happened there. And Peryn of the Archive, watching, understood that he had been wrong about the order of the world in one particular that changed everything: he had thought the fault in the eight would break *away* from the god, would be the one who at last screamed and clawed the bronze and refused. He had not imagined that the fault could break *toward* it — that the weakest stone was not the most afraid but the most *ambitious*, the one who wanted, out of the ruin of everything else, to be chosen; and that the god's mercy and the god's ruin were the same act, the same turning of the same eye, and that you could not pray for the one without receiving the other, because to a god there was no difference, there had never been a difference, the difference was a thing that lived only in the small burning selves that asked.

"Now we know," Peryn said, to no one, in the changed silence after. "It was never going to eat us. We were always going to hand ourselves in."

## The Word Finds the Crack

While the fault broke inward, the word broke out.

It went the way Peryn's own fault-water went — not by force, only by never letting up. The doors of the Hall of Nine Keys had received the rams and the jacks and the great forge-drills the way the sea receives a thrown stone, and the Foundry Commission's engineers, who did not give up easily on a thing made of metal, had at last done the one thing engineers do when a door will not open: they had gone at the *frame*. Not the bronze, which was hopeless, but the ancient stone the bronze was set in, chipping and coring and sounding it by lamplight in shifts, looking for the place where the founders' work had, in four hundred years of settling, grown a flaw. And in the coring, on some night no more marked than the rest, a drill-crew's long bit came through — not into the hall, the stone was a full arm thick yet, but into a *fault* in the stone, a hair-crack running who knew how deep, an old healed wound in the founders' masonry — and up through that crack, faint, on the dead cave-air of the sealed hall, came a sound.

It was a voice. It was, though the drill-crew could not have named it and would argue about it after in the low way men argue about a thing that has frightened them, the voice of a man saying the same few words over and over, the way a man says a prayer or a name of the dead — and the words, when the crew put their ears to the cored stone and held their breath and the drills went still, were these: *it is a god. it is a god. we have chained a god.*

It was Aldric. Aldric ban Sorel, who had come to vote *enemy* and had knelt instead and named the thing, and who in the shapeless days since had knelt on unmoving, first of the four kneelings and now the longest, and who had at some point in the wearing begun to *say* it — not to the well, which needed no telling, and not to the eight, who had stopped listening, but to himself, into the marble, the way a man holds onto the one true thing he has left by saying it and saying it until the saying is the last of him still standing. *It is a god. We have chained a god.* And the founders' stone, which had held the word inside for so long, had grown its one small flaw, and the engineers had found it, and now the word went up the drill-hole and out into the ear of a plain drill-crew foreman named — the ballads would keep this name, of all the names, keep it and not know why — named Corse, who took his ear from the stone and looked at his crew and did not, for a long moment, say anything at all.

And then he did the thing that no bronze door could have stopped and no council could have forbidden, because it was smaller than any law: he told his brother. And his brother, who ran a cart to the fish-market before light, told the woman who kept the market scales, because it was too large a thing to hold; and she told the deputy she reported the day's tolls to, because it might matter and she was afraid; and the deputy, who sat in the shadow-council that ran the city while the Nine were sealed, did not say it in the sitting, because to say it in the sitting would be to make it real — but he thought about it all through the sitting, while the deputies resolved once again to watch and wait and send to the other cities for counsel, and that night he told his wife.

This is how a word gets out. Not through the door. Through the *frame*. Not by the force that batters the bronze, but by the flaw the batterers open in the stone around it, and then by the oldest engine in the Free Cities, older than the Hall, older than the Long Cold, older than any law the founders wrote or threw away: one frightened person, unable to hold a thing alone, handing it to the next. By the time the beacon had burned a week on the north wall over the waiting wing, there was not a quarter of the city where you could not hear it, if you knew how to listen — under the ordinary noise of a frightened place doing its frightened business, a second sound, faint and spreading, passed ear to ear and never once said aloud in any room where saying it could be written down: *they say the council did not judge it. They say a warden knelt. They say it is a god, and we have chained a god, and no one has opened the doors because there is no one left in there to open them.*

The deputies felt it before they understood it — the way the city had felt the second wyrm as a deepening in the cloud before it was a shape. They felt the mood of the place change under them, the fear going from the clean cold fear of a caged beast to some other fear with a warmth in it, a wrongness, a *leaning*; felt the market-crowds slow and thicken near the sealed heart of the city as though drawn; felt the beacon-watchers on the north wall begin, some of them, to turn and look not out at the hill but *in*, toward the Hall, toward the thing they could not see. And they did what deputies do. They resolved, in careful language, to say nothing; to *deny the rumor of any irregularity in the deliberations of the Nine*; to assure the cities that the council sat in ordinary session and would render its lawful verdict in ordinary time. They wrote this down, and sealed it, and sent it by rider to the other magistracies, a lie in grey-and-iron wax going out across the plain to meet the beacon-fires coming the other way — the beacon crying *a wyrm, a wyrm* and the wax swearing *all is ordinary*, the two messages passing each other in the night on the same roads, and the country receiving both, and believing, in the way frightened countries do, the worse one.

## The Boy Hears the Chord

At the gate, through all of it, the boy of the night-watch kept his hours.

He did not hear the word that was spreading through the city, or if he heard it he did not understand it to be about the same thing his own two hands knew; the city's rumor and the boy's warm hollow in the ash had not yet found each other, and would not, for a while. But on the night the deputies sealed their lie in the wax and sent it out — the night, though he could not have known it, that the word of the kneeling reached its high tide in the city behind him and Vessa found her peace in the temple beneath it — on that night the boy, kneeling in the dark at the gate with the beacon red on the wall above him, heard the notes change again.

They had been three, and going toward a fourth. He had felt the third one, the near one, the egg's own note under his palms, rise from *answering* to *calling*. Now, in the deep of the sealed city's worst night, kneeling over the warm ash while a woman he would never meet burned quietly to peace at the lip of a well he would never see and a word he could not read ran ear to ear through the streets at his back, the boy felt the fourth note *come in.* It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the first light comes; it came up not through the ash alone but through the whole ground, through the gate-stones and the wall and the far sealed heart of the city, through the north where the wing lay waiting; and it was not a note the way the others were notes. It was the thing the other three had been *for*. It was the chord closing — the low far one from the Hall, and the near far one from the hill, and the close warm one from the ash, all three of them held now and steadied and *gathered* by a fourth that was not louder than they were but *deeper*, that came in under them the way the floor comes in under a house, and made of the three struck notes not a chord any longer but a single sounding thing, whole, and patient, and beginning — the boy understood this with his hands and not his head, and did not run — *beginning to move.*

The ash pressed outward against his palms, slow and enormous, the size of the dark testing its walls.

And far off, past the wall, past the killing-ground, on the bald hill in the north, the second wyrm — which had lain a full week without stirring, head laid low, wings struck and lashed, waiting with the patience of a planet for a thing no one on the walls could name — lifted its head.

---

*So the fault in the eight breaks at last, and breaks the wrong way — toward the god and not away, for the weakest stone was not the most afraid but the most hungry to be chosen, and Vessa of the River Guilds gets exactly the regard she demands and is burned smooth by it into the temple's first peace. And the word of the kneeling, kept so long behind the bronze, finds at last the flaw in the frame the batterers opened, and goes out the oldest way there is, ear to ear, one frightened soul to the next, until the city leans toward its own sealed heart and the deputies seal a lie against it. And at the gate the fourth note comes in under the other three and gathers them, and the wing on the northern hill lifts its head; and in the seventh chapter the word that got out must be answered — by the shadow-council that lied, by the nine cities the beacon woke, and by the thing at the gate that has, at last, all the notes it was waiting for, and begins to move.*
