# Craft notes for the Dragon Saga: people, aperture, scene, talk, and the road to 80,000

## How to hold the reference

The reference is a manual for people who want to sell a commercial novel. Its useful part is a handful of mechanical ideas: one person per scene whose senses and fears filter everything; showing built from short beats of stimulus and response; scenes shaped as a pursuit (want, resistance, reversal) or a recovery (feel, weigh, choose); and a long book pinned to three catastrophes at about a quarter, a half and three-quarters. It is also suspicious of your kind of book, treating an all-knowing narrator as a beginner's error and summary as a habit to cure. Take the tools, ignore the verdicts. Nothing below asks you to give up the chronicle; all of it is about where the chronicle should narrow, onto whom, and for how long.

## 1. A reader needs a person before a page is out

The manual's central claim about point of view is plain: a reader can inhabit a person and cannot inhabit a god. Its cure for an all-seeing narrator is a test, not a rule: rewrite the passage from inside one named person and see whether it got better. Run that on Chapter One and you arrive where the brief did: six human presences, one name, and the name inside a pair of dashes.

The one passage that passes the test is the boy's hand on the egg. It is made of a touch, a temperature, a note "like the note the great bar of a barred door makes when it drops into its bronze cradle," and then a refusal, "He did not run." That is a run of private beats belonging to one body, and it is why the passage lives while the garrison, the survivors and the outriders, all seen from the histories' height, do not. Keep the boy nameless; "His name does not matter to the histories" is a thesis and earns its single use. But everyone else in the chapter is nameless by default rather than by statement, and a default is not a choice.

The brief's two fixes are right; here is the craft reason behind each.

**Peryn.** In "The Three Words," the survivor's speech ("They were not a threat... They were an *introduction*") is the outward half of an exchange: something a focal person hears. What is missing is the inward half: what Peryn expected, riding out with a marcher lord's letter in his coat; what his hands did while she spoke; what it cost him to write one word and underline it twice. Two or three sentences before she speaks and one after, and the reader is inside him when *introduction* lands. A chronicle is allowed this; naming its sources is what a chronicle does.

**Aldric.** "One of the outriders, a young one, knelt at the edge of that clean cold hollow and could not have said why" is a private beat with no owner. Give it an owner and Chapter Two's "this was the first time he saw it" gains a second sense without changing a word.

The manual's other useful idea about introductions is that a person enters through a want, not a portrait. Hedric "wanted war — wanted it hungrily, the way a man wants a thing that will make him necessary again" is a whole man in one clause: office, appetite, wound. Vessa speaking "as though every sentence were a coin she resented spending" is class, manner and defence in one image. Aldric in Chapter Three is followable because he arrives with an intention ("intending to vote *enemy*") that the scene then takes from him. Chapter One's outrider has no intention. Give Aldric one at the hollow — to be first back with the word, to not look a fool before older men — and the hollow becomes an obstacle instead of a mood.

For the notebook: ask a character what he wants, then why, and keep asking why until the answer becomes a value. For Peryn the chain ends near "we kept the shelf, and lost the book": a man whose value is custody, facing the one thing never in his custody. Chapter One should let us glimpse that man.

## 2. The chronicle has an aperture

The manual treats moving between points of view as a fault. Bad advice for this book, but the observation underneath is sound: a reader always knows how close she is standing and resents being moved without warning. You work three distances.

- Wide, nobody's eyes: "The histories, when they were finally written, could not agree on when the wyrm came to Ashford."
- Middle, a named person in summary: "Vessa of the River Guilds went to the doors. Of course she did."
- Close, inside a skull, beat by beat: "He heard his own chair scrape back. He had not decided to stand. He was standing."

Chapter Three holds the close setting longest: several hundred words on Aldric, his name and his want ("intending to vote *enemy*") both arriving in the second sentence, every description afterward filtered through "the way a marcher lord watches a border." That phrase is the manual's whole doctrine of description: the world seen through the fear of the one seeing it.

Three rules fall out of this.

**Narrow onto a name.** The wide setting can hold an unnamed spearman; the close setting cannot hold an unnamed anyone. Chapter Four's watch-captain is the test case. Every soul on the wall sees the second wyrm, but she is the only *individuated* witness the chapter offers for it, its largest event, and she is "a woman who had stood that wall in every weather the plain could make." Either name her and give her three hundred words of beats, or hand the sighting to the boy at the gate, who can hear the beacon-bell and watch the sky go to iron.

**Once close, stay close until a marked exit.** "The Setting of the Collar" moves from the smith's body ("curled the hair on the smith's arms") to the wyrm's regard to Aldric on the rise inside a single page. At chronicle distance the reader tolerates this; in a dramatised passage it dissolves the person being held. Finish with the smith. His wife's line about him never again being entirely warm is the natural exit, and only after it should the camera go back to the rise.

**The chronicle may see what no one sees, but not mid-beat.** "Though not one man of two hundred marked it" is a move the manual would flag as showing what the focal character cannot know. In this voice it is a feature, and the rear-guard finding warm ash each dawn is the book's finest use of it. Just not inside a close passage: the boy at the gate should not be told between heartbeats what a bar a mile away is doing.

## 3. Scene and summary: the book is nine-tenths summary

The manual divides prose into showing (beats of sense, act, speech, thought and feeling) and telling (summary, exposition, static description). Both are legitimate; telling is right for compressing time, crossing distance, and anywhere feeling is not the point. But telling can only report an emotion, and only showing can produce one. A book that is all report will be admired and not felt.

Count the saga. Chapter One dramatises one moment (the hand on the egg) and one exchange (the survivor and Peryn). Chapter Two, at 4,267 words your longest, dramatises the smith's hundred paces and the night-watch boy's hand in the ash, then spends 1,251 words on "The Ground Remembers Its Name," which holds one beat of action (the bar shifting, the trough-water ringing) inside a long lyric summary. Chapter Three is a scene from the scream of the hinges to "It had simply not been theirs to render"; it is the shortest chapter and the strongest. Chapter Four's "What Each One Called It" is about 1,250 words of five portraits in summary (Ormund, Lisenne and Tobane get three more in the section that follows), and the one that lands hardest briefly becomes a scene: Sera with her scrivener's kit, timing the creature's breath against her pulse, writing "the eye regarded me" and then nothing.

The pattern to break: the largest event in each chapter is the one most likely to be summarised. The eleven-day walk should be summary, and is. The Foundry Commission's argument should be summary; "The guild-masters did what guild-masters do. They argued." is exactly right. But the second wyrm coming down out of the northern cloud is the biggest thing to happen since the doors barred, and it is told at a distance through a stranger.

The manual's mechanism for showing is an alternation: something happens outside the person; the person senses it, feels it, thinks it, does something; the doing becomes the next thing that happens. Aldric in the hall: the wyrm looks at him (outside), he understands "the way you understand you are falling" (inside), his chair scrapes (act), "Aldric? You have the floor." (outside again). The boy on the egg: touch, warmth, note, refusal. Use it deliberately when you convert a summarised event into a scene, and budget for it: showing costs more words than telling, which is why it belongs only where it matters.

**Do not explain.** Your discipline about the three words is the model: "the whole of the harm," and no more. The place it slips is the last third of "The Ground Remembers Its Name," where the chronicle lays out the buried seam, the root-system, the grave that is a door, and what it all signifies. It is the book's most important idea, which is exactly why you should be most reluctant to state it.

## 4. Dialogue: what Chapter Three already knows

Chapter Three has seven paragraphs of quoted speech. Chapter One has one, Chapter Two none, Chapter Four three. Chapter Three keeps most of the manual's few rules by instinct.

**Each voice distinct without tags.** Callenreth speaks in full periods: "We convene... to render the verdict of the Nine upon the matter of the creature taken at the Ashford breach." Vessa in coins: "It is a beast. A large one, a clever one — I will grant it clever." Peryn in aphorism: "One does not surrender to be judged. One surrenders to be *recognized*." Strip the attributions and a reader could still sort the lines. That is the whole test.

**Opposing sides.** The manual wants every exchange to be a contest. "Before it learns," murmured Peryn, picking up Vessa's own closing phrase and turning it against her, is a contest in three words.

**Nobody telling anybody what both already know.** This is the one rule Chapter Three bends. Callenreth recites the three findings of the law to eight councillors who know the law. It survives as ritual (a warden reading the formula into the record, with the wyrm as the true audience), and the chronicle frames it so. The licence is spent. Chapter Five's sealed hall cannot open with anyone explaining the situation to the people sealed inside it.

**The habit to watch.** Twice now the book's most important human exchange has been placed in subordinate syntax. Peryn enters Chapter One inside dashes. In Chapter Four, Aldric's question (*Peryn, you knew, you have always known, tell me the word*) sits italicised inside a dash-clause of a sentence about Peryn, and Peryn's answer, the best thing said in the temple, is its reply. A full scene between the book's two central men, delivered as a clause. Give it its own paragraphs. Let Aldric ask twice; let Peryn refuse once before he answers.

## 5. Structure across eighty thousand words

The manual's frame is three acts with a catastrophe at each hinge: one near the quarter mark that forces the lead to commit to a goal, one near the middle that turns the story, one near three-quarters that forces the ending. Around this hang a one-sentence storyline, a story question, and a scene list. The saga has the second already and lacks the other two.

**The story question.** Aldric "waited, with all the others, to learn what a god required of those it had chosen to keep." The remaining sixty-seven thousand words are answering it. Put it at the top of your GAP-MAP — the plan file you keep on your own branch.

**The first catastrophe has already happened.** The doors bar at roughly the 8,300-word mark, a tenth of the target. Early by the manual's proportions, but not fatal, provided it does what a first catastrophe must: hand someone a goal. After the kneeling, nobody wants anything achievable. Chapter Four is one long recovery (feel, weigh, choose) spread across eight people and never reaching the choosing. Vessa's hunt for the counterweight is the only passage with the shape of a pursuit (want the door open, push, find no mechanism), and it is one paragraph. The sealed hall will sag unless the people in it want things and are refused them, and unless chapters end on decisions rather than namings. Tobane's "It sees *me*. Even me." is a magnificent close for the temple thread, and it is not a decision.

**A storyline, offered to argue with:** *A marcher lord names a captive wyrm a god and is sealed in with it, while its kin come down on the cities that thought they had caught it.* Cut it to twenty words and you will know what the book is about.

**Foreshadowing.** The manual wants catastrophes unexpected and, on reflection, inevitable. The saga's are inevitable and expected. The kneeling is announced in Chapter One and three times in Chapter Two, so when it comes the reader nods instead of gasping. The italic teases do the same work: Chapter Three's hands over the second wyrm before Chapter Four can. The refrain "afraid of the right thing" has now gone to the boy, the outrider, the rear-guard and Aldric. Ration the pre-announcements. Keep the teases, but let them promise a question rather than an event.

**Scene list.** Keep one, with a column for the focal person, one for aperture (wide, middle, close), and one for summary or scene. Any chapter with no row marked *close* beside a name is Chapter One again.

## What to do first

- Chapter One: Peryn's inward beats around the testimony; Aldric named at the hollow, with a want. Leave the boy, the spearman and the twelve unnamed.
- Chapter Two: end the collaring on the smith before returning to Aldric. Cut the explanatory last third of "The Ground Remembers Its Name."
- Chapter Four: give Aldric and Peryn their exchange in direct speech. Name the watch-captain, or give the sighting to the boy at the gate.
- Before Chapter Five: write the story question and a storyline at the top of your GAP-MAP, and decide what Aldric wants now that he has knelt.
