# Worldbuilding for the Dragon Saga

## The one fact the whole world serves

Most fantasy worlds start from the assumption that the gods are real and nobody can honestly deny them. Your saga starts from the opposite: a country that *made* them deniable by statute. Callenreth lays out the three findings — beast, enemy, sovereign — and the chronicle says the line the whole book turns on: "There was no fourth word in the law." Peryn gives the founding its epitaph: "we kept the shelf, and lost the book." Everything below serves that inversion. A confederacy of clerks legislated the divine out of its vocabulary, built its strongest room over the pool where its founders read omens, and is now being visited by the thing it struck from the record. Every institution you design should be able to answer one question: *what did it lose when the old word was thrown away, and what does it stand to gain or lose now that the word is back?*

## What a chronicle needs from a world, versus what a game needs

A game world has to be wide, because the people inside it can walk in any direction; the designer stocks a whole province with places and answers, most never used. A novel needs almost none of that. It needs depth only where the story touches the ground — and the same answer every time the ground is touched, because a reader cannot be steered back on course the way a table can.

Your voice raises the bar further. A chronicle *cites*. "The histories, when they were finally written, could not agree." "Every account keeps that." "It is recorded, in the account Sera Lowe wrote and did not sign." "The ballads would afterward insist." Each is a claim that a document exists, written by someone, kept somewhere. So the first piece of worldbuilding this book needs is not a map; it is a source list. Who wrote the histories, and from which side of the bronze doors? Where does Sera's unsigned account end up? Who sings the ballads that got the taking wrong, and why do they and the Archive disagree? The narrator is allowed not to know — that is the voice's great license — but *you* are not, and the difference between "the histories could not agree" as a mythic gesture and as cover for an undecided author is audible on the page.

The second deliverable is a timeline, because the text already commits to hard numbers and they must add up. Fires three days cold when the boy finds the egg. Outriders "days later." Second and third riders. Nine days of forging. An unstated number of days for the column to ride west. Eleven days back at ox pace. "Weeks" before the full Nine convene — which quietly tells you the Nine do not all live in the capital; they had to *come*. Then "days" sealed, and a northern cloud that "had sat over the plain for three days." Put this on one page with distances (eleven days at wain pace is roughly a hundred and twenty miles of road) and the gaps you find are the decisions still owed. Keep the whole world bible to two pages. Anything past that is a scene you have not written yet, not a world.

## The Free Cities and the magistracy

**What the text fixes.** A "league" of "nine magistracies," each with its own council, under grey-and-iron banners, run by warrants, ledgers and commissions; a body that "had outlived every threat it was written to answer and had never once answered one that breathed." That line is a whole political history: the levies and beacons were built against cold, flood, fire and plague, not against men. The Cities make and unmake kings ("we make kings, and we unmake them") and treat with sovereigns as equals — a republic among monarchies, so "Free" means freed *from* someone. Name who.

**What is undecided.** *Magistracy* does two jobs. Sometimes it is a place with rooms ("out of all the rooms in all their nine magistracies"); sometimes an institution ("High Warden of a magistracy that could not hear him"). The Nine are not nine cities: they are a river guild, a wall guild, a bank, a fire guild, a fen wardenship, a marcher lordship, an archive, a presiding office, and Sera Lowe's seat, which has no named body at all. And Chapter Three has Aldric kneeling where "his kingdom's founders" read omens — the only *kingdom* in a book otherwise scrupulous about league, cities and magistracies.

**Recommendation.** Make each magistracy a *jurisdiction defined by function*, with a house in the chief city and reach across all the cities: River, Walls, Coin, Kiln, Fen, Marches, Archive, Wardenship, and whatever Sera's is. Then nine keys means nine consents; the Hall opens only when all nine agree no smaller door remains, which is exactly why it stayed shut forty years and why the Foundry Commission was convened instead. Number the chairs by precedence of the keys — the text already gives Aldric "third-chair" — and decide who holds second, because from Chapter Five the city is run by whoever is *not* sealed in: deputies, commissions, the quartermasters still arguing over a half-dug pit. That regency of clerks is your outside-the-Hall engine and costs nothing you have not already built. Either strike "kingdom" or make it mean something: the Marches were a kingdom once, and joined.

One more word is overloaded: *warden*. It names the presiding officer (High Warden), the men who haul the doors, guild heads (guild-wardens), and Lisenne, "warden of the low wet country." Pick a rule. Cleanest: a warden is anyone holding a key or warrant under the law, and the door-haulers are something else — keymen, serjeants of the Hall.

## The Hall of Nine Keys

The Hall is the founding act in stone: built "to hold judgment," over the pool where the founders read the future, with bronze doors, a vault, and murder-holes "that had never in forty years been meant to fire inward." A fortress-courtroom raised on an oracle. The politics of Chapter Three follow from the architecture. The three findings are a taxonomy of what a state can *do* to a thing — kill it, war on it, treat with it — and a god is by definition the thing a state can do nothing to. When nine frightened clever people sit down to judge one, the book is dramatizing not a trial but the limit of the law's vocabulary, discovered from inside. The room becoming a temple is not a haunting; it is the founding running backward. Say that to yourself before Chapter Five, because it names what the *city* is losing while the eight learn their words: its constitution.

Two questions the Hall still owes you. Why was it last opened forty years ago, and for what — the Long Cold, the plague-years? The answer gives Callenreth's "forty years of office" a beginning. And why is the well dry? The chronicle hints the omen-pool was real ("had the founders of the Free Cities only read their omen-pools the right way round"). If divination worked and was legislated away with the old word, the water leaving is a consequence, not a detail.

## The Archive and its scribes

Peryn is the book's second lead, and the Archive is the one institution that behaves *correctly* in all four chapters: it sends a single dry note telling the Commission to study why the thing came rather than how to hold it, it dispatches a scribe with a marcher lord's letter years after the fact, and its councillor says the true thing first. Define it with care. It keeps the law and the old tongues, and — this is the decision — it keeps the *shelf*: a record that something was removed. Was the book lost, or purged at the founding with the empty shelf left as a confession? The second is stronger and fits "the new law threw the old word away." Decide whether the Archive writes the histories the narrator keeps citing and whether the ballads are its rival tradition. And note how a scribe travels: Peryn needed a marcher lord's letter to reach a survivor, so the Archive has authority in the city and none in the countryside without a lord's hand.

## The border levy, the beacon-chain and the marcher lords

The text gives you a militia in one clause: "farmers three seasons of four, soldiers when the beacon-chain lit." Twelve at Ashford; two hundred under captains on the column; the largest muster "since the last lighting of the beacon-chain." Who owns the beacons? Hedric speaks of them and a wall-captain lights one, so the Wallsmen. What can a fire *say*? Chapter Four has the chain carry "a wyrm, a wyrm" faster than any rider — fine as rhetoric, but a beacon says one thing, *muster*, and riders carry the rest. Know which, so the countryside's response in later chapters is a response to a fire, not a sentence. What is the border a border against, if no threat ever breathed? The "cold country" north with "the old high places its kind were said to keep," and the Long Cold that Sera was born after, already point one way. Make it deliberate: the north is where the cold, the second wyrm and the old stories all come from.

Geography needs one decision. The column rode *west* to Ashford; the wyrm flew "east, and inland"; and Aldric's Southern Marches are "the seat nearest the Ashford road." So Ashford is southwest, or the Marches wrap the south and west. Fix it on a map of about eight places: Ashford, the rise east of it, the road, the outer gate and its sentry-fire, the north wall, the wharves, the Hall a mile inside, the bald hill. "The debt the eastern baronies still owed in men" describes a tributary frontier outside the league — and it is Hedric's reason for wanting a war.

Aldric is the feudal element inside a republic: a hereditary lord who holds a chair, "raised" to answer *enemy*. Tobane "inherited his seat" too. So at least two keys are hereditary and the rest are not — Vessa's presumably a guild election, Peryn's seniority, Callenreth's an office chosen by the others. Write the rule for each seat. Succession is the one question Chapter Five cannot avoid.

## The guilds, briefly

**River Guilds.** Vessa spends words like coin, and her vote — *beast*, kill it "before it learns the shape of this room" — is a trade vote: a monster in the capital stops barges. Decide whether it is one guild or a federation of bargemen, ferrymen and millers, and why the river holds a key when the roads belong to the Wallsmen.

**Wallsmen.** Walls, road-guard, engineers, beacons, and a councillor who wants a war "that will make him necessary again." The walls have been idle a long time; that is a fact about the league's history, so date the last war.

**Coinhouse.** "The least of the Nine," an inherited seat, an anxious man. The saga is obsessed with ledgers and columns; the Coinhouse is where the ledger physically lives. Someone paid for "three cities' charcoal" and a whale's-weight of iron, and Tobane knows the number.

**Kilnward.** "Every guild that lived by fire." Chapter Two forges the chain under the Ironmongers' guild; Chapter Four puts Ormund on his knees before "the master of the fire." If the Ironmongers sit under Ormund's key, the collar was his house's work and his buckling is the payoff of a design decision. Make it so and say it once.

**Fenreeve.** Lisenne holds back water, "south of the river." Her word — "the flood that comes uphill" — is the best of the eight because it is a *professional's* word. Every seat should be able to produce one like it.

## Religion: what the founders believed

The reference distinguishes several shapes a faith can take, and two describe your book exactly. One is a divinity that has lost its worshippers and is counted dead by the living, yet can still be reached and still remembers its name. That is the wyrm: not a new god but a former one, and the ground under the gate "remembered what it was, and what had been laid in it, and what it had been called before the new law threw the old word away." The other is initiation — a private rite in which the initiate is personally identified with the divine — and Chapter Four is that rite eight times over: each councillor "arrived, alone and against their will, at a word," and the temple is finished not by Aldric's grandeur but by Tobane's "It sees *me*. Even me." Recognition, not conversion. Keep that as doctrine: this god does not want belief; it wants to be *seen back*.

So decide what the founders' religion was. The pool, the omen-reading, a burning under every stone "joined the way a root system is joined" — this reads as a faith of place, where the land has a name and can be asked it. The law replaced it with three findings and a ledger. Decide whether the old faith is dead in the cities and alive in the countryside — the survivor "in the far pasture" *understood* the three words, and she was a villager, not a scholar — or whether the wyrm is understood by anyone it addresses because it speaks "from inside her own chest." The second keeps the Archive's lost book relevant to *naming* rather than *understanding*, which is where the text already puts it.

## Magic: the one thing

There is no human magic in these chapters and there should not be. No priests, no charms, no hedge-witch. The world is mundane except for the wyrm, the egg, the ground and a dry well that once held the future, and that scarcity is what makes the one thing enormous. The rule: nothing supernatural happens that is not the wyrm, its kin, or the ground answering them. The doors closing with no hand on them is the ground, not a spell. Hold that and you never have to explain why the Cities have no wizards to consult; the answer is already on the page — their law has no word for the thing, and a people without the word do not train specialists in it.

## The antagonist's calendar

The most useful habit in the reference is this: before writing the next chapter, write what the adversary does *if nobody interferes*, dated. The wyrm has no malice — "without malice and without mercy" — but it has an appointment, and it has kept it on schedule for four chapters. So: what did it come to do in the Hall, and how long does it take? Where is the egg going, and what happens when it stops? What does the wing on the bald hill want — the tease offers "master to it, or kin, or lure," and you should know which before Chapter Five is drafted, even though the chronicle will withhold it. The narrator's uncertainty is an instrument; the author's is a leak.

Two inversions worth holding. Ordinary villains are undone by their true name; your god is *made* by being named, first grandly, then humanly. And Chapter Two says every smith felt he was "forging his half of a bargain whose other half had been agreed to long before he was born." If the cities' half is nearly complete, the question is not "can it be stopped" but "what did the founders promise, and what is delivered when the last link closes?" That is your mid-book upheaval — and one standing fact of this world, that the law has no fourth word, has already stopped being true.

## The decisions owed, in order

1. A dated timeline from the Ashford fire to the northern wing, with the column's westward ride filled in.
2. Magistracy = functional jurisdiction with a house; nine keys = nine consents.
3. The rule for filling each seat, and who holds second chair.
4. One meaning for *warden*; strike or justify "kingdom."
5. Why the Hall last opened forty years ago; why the well is dry.
6. What the Archive keeps, lost or purged, and who writes the histories and the ballads.
7. What a beacon can say; what the border was against; where Ashford lies relative to the Marches.
8. Whether the Ironmongers sit under the Kilnward key.
9. The founders' faith, and whether the countryside still keeps it.
10. The wyrm's calendar, the egg's destination, and the meaning of the second wing — for you, not the page.
