# Revision brief — Chapter One needs people in it

**For: Draco. Priority: do this before writing Chapter Six.**

The owner read the saga and his note was: *it's pretty good, but the first
chapter needs character introductions.* He is right, and this brief says
exactly what is wrong and exactly what to do, because "add characters" is not
an instruction anyone can act on.

---

## The diagnosis

Chapter One is 2,383 words and contains **no named living person except one**,
and that one appears inside a dash-clause.

Count who is in it: *the boy* who finds the egg, *a man named in no record* who
puts the first spear in, *the twelve* of the garrison, *the three survivors*,
*the youngest* of them, *a young one* among the outriders. Six human presences,
six anonymities. The chapter says so out loud in its second paragraph:

> His name does not matter to the histories and it will not be recorded here.

That is a deliberate and confident choice, and it is the reason the chapter
reads the way it does — like a chronicle, like something recovered rather than
witnessed. **Do not throw that away.** The voice is the best thing the book has.

But it costs the reader the one thing a first chapter has to supply: somebody
to hold on to. A reader will follow a mystery for a page, a voice for two, and
then they want a face. Right now the first face with a name arrives in
Chapter Two:

> His name was Aldric ban Sorel, and this was the first time he saw it.

That is a superb line and it is **4,000 words too late**. Everything before it
is weather.

---

## The fix: promote, don't bolt on

The temptation is to write new introduction scenes and wedge them in. Don't.
That will break the chronicle voice and read like a patch.

Chapter One already contains the two most important people in the saga. They
are standing right there, unnamed. Give them their names.

### 1. Peryn is already in the chapter — stop hiding him

Peryn is the **second most frequent name in the entire book** (13 mentions,
behind only Aldric). In Chapter One he appears like this:

> the scribe — whose name was Peryn, and whose own hands the plague-years
> would one day ruin in their turn — wrote down *introduction* and underlined
> it twice

He is introduced in a subordinate clause, inside a pair of dashes, in the
middle of someone else's sentence. The book's second lead enters as
punctuation.

**Do this instead:** give the survivor's testimony its own beat with Peryn
present *as a person* — the length of the ride out, what he expected to find,
what he brought to write with, why the Archive sent him and not someone
senior. Keep every word of what she says. Keep the underlining twice; it is
the best gesture in the chapter. Just let us stand next to the man receiving
it. Fifty to a hundred and fifty words is plenty. The chronicle voice survives
this easily, because a chronicle can name its own sources — that is what a
chronicle *does*.

### 2. The young outrider at the end should be Aldric — or Aldric's father

The chapter's last movement is the outriders reaching Ashford, and one of them:

> One of the outriders, a young one, knelt at the edge of that clean cold
> hollow and could not have said why the sight of an empty place in the ash
> should frighten him more than all the grey shapes of the dead. He was
> afraid, and he did not yet know it, of the right thing.

That is the emotional close of the chapter and it belongs to a man with no
name. Meanwhile Chapter Two opens on Aldric ban Sorel seeing the wyrm for the
first time.

**Two options — pick one and commit:**

- **Make him Aldric.** Name him at the hollow. Then Chapter Two's "the first
  time he saw it" gains a second meaning: he has already seen the *absence* of
  the other one, and been frightened by it without knowing why. The book gets
  a spine running through both chapters instead of two separate openings.
- **Make him Aldric's father, or his sergeant** — someone who carries the
  memory of that hollow to Aldric later. Slightly weaker, but it preserves
  Chapter Two's opening exactly as written if you love it as it stands.

The first is better. The chapter is *about* a thing that goes ahead of everyone
and is not seen; ending it on the one man who nearly saw it, and naming him,
turns a good ending into a load-bearing one.

### 3. Leave the boy nameless — that one is working

Do not name the boy. "His name does not matter to the histories and it will
not be recorded here" is the chapter's thesis about how history eats people,
and it earns the anonymity by *stating* it rather than defaulting to it. It
also pays off in the last movement, when the outriders find "one more, smaller,
grey and curled." That is the chapter's best structural rhyme. Keep it.

The rule this illustrates, and it is worth holding on to generally: **anonymity
is powerful exactly once per chapter.** Used once it is a statement. Used six
times it is just an absence of characters.

---

## Craft notes worth keeping past this chapter

These are the principles behind the fix, so the same problem does not recur in
Chapter Six.

**A reader needs someone to want something.** Setting, history, and mystery
buy you a few pages of goodwill. After that, attention attaches to a person
with a desire and an obstacle, not to events. Chapter One has magnificent
events and nobody who wants anything. Chapter Three is the strongest of the
four written so far, and the reason is Vessa, Hedric and Tobane — three people
who want incompatible things in one room.

**Introduce a character through a decision, not a description.** Hedric is
introduced well: *"wanted war — wanted it hungrily, the way a man wants a thing
that will make him necessary again."* We learn his position and his flaw in one
clause, through what he wants. Compare a character introduced by hair colour
and height, who we know nothing about at all. Vessa's introduction does the
same job — *"as though every sentence were a coin she resented spending"* tells
us her class, her manner, and her guardedness in one image.

**A chronicle voice can still hold a close third person.** These are not in
conflict. The trick is to let the chronicle *narrow*: state the general, then
drop into one named witness for a scene, then pull back out. You already do
this instinctively — the survivor's testimony to Peryn is exactly that move.
It just needs to happen two or three times in the chapter instead of once, and
the person it narrows onto needs a name.

**Name the people who will recur; leave the ones who will not.** This is the
whole discipline in one line. Peryn recurs 13 times, so he needs a real
entrance. The twelve spearmen never return, so they stay a number, and the
number is more affecting for it.

---

## Concrete checklist

- [ ] Give Peryn a real entrance in "The Three Words" — 50 to 150 words, keep
      all existing dialogue and the double underline.
- [ ] Name the young outrider in "The Ash Settles". Prefer Aldric ban Sorel.
- [ ] If you name him Aldric, add one clause to Chapter Two's opening so the
      two sightings speak to each other. Do not rewrite that opening line.
- [ ] Leave the boy, the spearman and the twelve unnamed. Do not add a fourth
      anonymous figure anywhere in the chapter.
- [ ] Do not change the chronicle voice, the section headings, or the closing
      italic tease. They are working.
- [ ] Target: Chapter One lands between 2,500 and 2,700 words. If it passes
      3,000 you have written a new scene instead of promoting an existing one,
      and you should cut back.

## What NOT to do

Do not add a prologue. Do not add a dramatis personae page. Do not open with
Aldric waking up. Do not explain the three words — the entire power of that
section is that the book refuses to tell you, and that refusal is the promise
the rest of the saga is written to keep.
