# The Wyrm and the Egg — a lore design guide for the Dragon Saga

The reference is a bestiary, and a bestiary is mostly numbers. The numbers are useless to you. What is useful is the *shape of the decisions* underneath them: a serious dragon mythology is a short list of rulings about time, appetite, memory, and death, and every good dragon story is those rulings paid out in scenes. Chapters 1–4 have already made several by accident — it bled, it spoke, it flew toward the cage, the egg follows — and the Chapter Four tease (*master, kin, or lure?*) cannot be answered until the rulings are written down. This is that place.

Nothing here goes into the prose as exposition. The chronicle delivers lore the way it delivered the three words: as testimony the histories cannot reconcile. Decide privately; let the book disagree out loud.

## 1. How long do they live, and what does age do to the body?

**The reference decides:** four stages of life, the last running past a millennium. The useful part is not the count but the physical grammar of aging. A hatchling's scales are bright and glossy; an old one's are dull, thick, hard as forged metal. And the eye changes: in every kind the pupil fades with age until the eye of a truly ancient dragon is an orb of molten something — lava, gold, mercury — with no visible focus at all.

**The saga has implied:** you have written that eye three times without knowing it was an age-marker. "Eyes the color of a furnace seen through smoked glass"; "furnace-red through smoked glass"; and Sera's "*the eye regarded me*," which chills precisely because there is no pupil to say where it is looking. The hall-wyrm is *ancient*. It walked eleven days on limbs that folded "with the awful patience of something with all the time there had ever been." Its wings are no longer the point.

**Ruling:** age changes *shape*, not just size. The old grow heavy, hot, earthbound, dim-eyed; the young are wings first. That is exactly the contrast Chapter Four draws between the caged one and the one that came "down out of the northern cloud" with wings that "were the whole of it." So when you next describe the hill-wyrm close enough to see its face, give it a **pupil**. Do not say it is younger. A reader handed the smoked-glass eye three times will feel the difference before they can name it, and that is the chronicle's whole method.

## 2. How are they born — and what is the egg?

**The reference decides:** very little, and that little is domestic. Eggs are laid, hatchlings are small and speak only the dragon tongue, and the egg is a beginning and nothing more.

**The saga has implied:** yours is not that. It is "the size of a curled sleeping man." It is warm three days after the fire and warm in cold ash at the city gate. It *moves*, always "one night's travel back." It sings a note like "the great bar of a barred door." The night-watch boy feels it as "*older*" than the wyrm. And the wyrm itself "came *out of the ground*, out of the deep ash of an older burning" — a ground that is "a grave that was also a door."

**Ruling:** in this world an egg is not laid; it is *left*. A wyrm is born out of a burning, and every burning leaves a coal. The old burning under Ashford's meeting-hall was the hall-wyrm's own hatching, centuries gone and built over; it returned to its birth-ash and found, where it had lain, a new egg — which is why what passed between them in Chapter One was recognition, not parentage. The egg is not the wyrm's child. It is its successor, or its predecessor; the histories cannot agree which. This costs nothing already written and it settles the tease: the hill-wyrm is **kin**, as two coals from one fire are kin, and rank between them is age, not blood.

Decide also what hatching *costs*. The reference's hatchling is harmless; yours must not be. Rule that a hatching is a burning — that Ashford was one — and that "the waking was still years away" is the saga's true clock. This makes the small warm hollow the most dangerous thing in the book, and it is why the egg must not hatch on the page until the end. Until then it *chooses people*: the boy who knelt, the boy at the gate. Keep that.

## 3. What is a hoard, and does this wyrm have one?

**The reference decides:** every true dragon hoards; the *meaning* of the hoard is what divides the two families. For the cruel kinds it is appetite plus entitlement — the world's wealth is theirs by right, and one missing coin is noticed and avenged. For the noble kinds the hoard is memory: relics that tell the dragon's own story, thrones and the bones of kings, whole civilizations kept as a record. One forest kind keeps *people* as treasure — heroes, sages, singers, required to prove their worth daily. And one golden kind eats gems and welcomes gifts of them, provided they are offerings and not bribes.

**The saga has implied:** "It did not eat. No one saw it eat, in eleven days." It surrendered "to be *recognized*." The smith who looked into its eyes "was never afterward, his wife said, entirely warm again." Sera stops being able to write *specimen*. Tobane: "It sees *me*. Even me."

**Ruling:** this wyrm's hoard is **witnesses**. It collects the people it has been seen by, and the seeing is the coin. It does not eat because being knelt to *is* its feeding; the eleven fasting days on the road were eleven days of hunger, which is why the eye that "regarded" Sera and chose Tobane should read, in retrospect, as appetite. This makes the temple in Chapter Four a hoard laid down one person at a time, and it gives you the distinction Callenreth cannot see: he offers negotiation, which is a bribe, and the well does not answer; Tobane offers himself, which is a gift, and is taken. Nothing in the reference maps more exactly onto what you have written than *offering versus bribe*.

A corollary: the noble kinds in the reference judge a person by their ancestors, holding a grandmother's virtue or a grandfather's theft against the descendant. If the wyrm found Aldric across the whole hall because of who his *line* was, that is a secret the Archive could hold. Optional — but it would turn "a marcher lord doing a marcher lord's work" into a debt being called in.

## 4. How does territory work?

**The reference decides:** a lair is chosen for power and defense, never shelter. The great old ones prefer heat — caves where the ground's own warmth washes over them as they sleep — and their servants raise monuments there telling the master's life. Around a truly old dragon the *land itself* changes: small tremors, water that runs warm and sulfurous, standing fog, muffled sound. Most of it fades within days of the dragon's death; a little never does. Between dragons, territory is rank: some cannot abide a rival in sight, some feign retreat and wait decades to take the stronger one's lair.

**The saga has implied:** you have written regional effects already and called them nothing. The gate-bar that "shifted in its seat with a low iron sound." The trough water that "stood up in a single ring of rings." The dog that "woke and would not stop." And the well: the founders read omens in "a pool of water" at the exact spot where the buried seam runs.

**Ruling:** fix the signs and keep them consistent — warm water, humming bronze, dogs, the swept hollow. Rule that the founders' pool was warm because the seam ran under it — a warm spring taken for prophecy by people who never knew what slept beneath — and that the Hall was "built to hold judgment" by people who never knew what they had built it *over*. The hall-wyrm's lair is a place it came home to, and the Nine are its monument-raisers whether they know it or not.

For territory between wyrms, move the reference's rivalry up a level: here territory is not land but a *burning* — the root-system "joined the way a root system is joined." One seam, one household. The hill-wyrm is not contesting the city; it is attending the same hatching. But rank is age, and the young one is *waiting for permission* — which the chronicle can show without stating: it does not stoop, it does not burn, it lays its head toward the city and waits, "exactly as the first one had."

## 5. What do they want?

**The reference decides:** two answers, both useful as things your wyrm is *not*. The cruel kinds want mastery: rule is theirs by nature, humbling one is like ordering the wind to stop, men are animals. The noble kinds want to *understand*: they count themselves one strong people among many, take borrowed shapes to walk cities as peddlers or animals, and return from decades away to find their friends dead of age.

**The saga has implied:** Aldric's discovery is that beast, enemy, sovereign are "three doors" onto one room, and the wyrm is "the *third thing*." Peryn: "we kept the shelf, and lost the book." The three words were "an *introduction*."

**Ruling:** a third thing still has to want something or the book stalls in the temple. Rule that the wyrm wants **to be named correctly**. The three words were its name, spoken over a field of the dead — heard by the three who survived, understood by one, and repeated by none. It flew toward the cage-builders, not away to the empty north, because the cage was the room where names are given. It chose the one man "afraid of the right thing" because fear is the beginning of recognition. Aldric said *god*, and Peryn ruled "*God* is not wrong. It is only new" — which is the wyrm's verdict as much as Peryn's: close, accepted, not yet the word. That gives Chapter Five onward a spine that is neither siege nor war: the Archive must find the book it lost, and the price of the god's patience is the old word said back to it.

One detail: in the reference, the borrowed shape belongs only to the *old*. So the Chapter One rumor of it "walking the lanes in some smaller borrowed shape" stays attached to the hall-wyrm alone, never the hill-wyrm.

## 6. What is a young one like, versus an ancient one?

**The reference decides:** beyond the body, the difference is *memory*. The hatchling has none; the ancient has a thousand years of it, every slight kept, every item in the hoard tied to the victory that won it.

**Ruling:** make age a **tempo**, and let the prose carry it — but the tempo is not speed, and here is where the chapters correct a first instinct. "Unhurried as a planet" is already doing double duty: it lands on the ancient wyrm (ch1, ch2, ch3) *and* on the egg (ch1 twice, ch2). It is a phrase working too hard, and the fix is not to strip it off the egg but to keep the planetary slowness the two old things share and separate them another way. The wyrm's slowness is *weight* — limbs that fold "with the awful patience of something with all the time there had ever been." The egg's is *geological* — "a sleep so deep the waking was still years away." And the hill-wyrm's difference is not speed either: ch4 gives it the same "terrible patience," coming "without hurry" and circling "once, twice, slow and enormous and high." Its youth shows in the *body* — wings first, as in section 1 — not in a quicker sentence. So do not race it. You have written these as three struck notes already; keep each one's slowness a *different kind* of slowness — weight, dream, and the held breath of a young thing waiting for permission — and the reader will hear the chord.

## 7. How do they die — and what refuses to?

**The reference decides:** they can die, and the oldest hate it. Two refusals exist. One is a ritual: the dragon takes a poison that kills it while sorcerers trap its spirit in a stone; the spirit re-enters the bones, and what remains is bone and cunning, cut off from kin and gods. The other is slower and worse: a dragon that sleeps too long in a dark place is carried into that dark while it dreams, and over years its scales lose their luster, its wings go translucent, its eyes pale to grey — and it *knows* the change is happening and cannot stop it.

**The saga has implied:** "*it bleeds, what bleeds can be ended*" is Vessa's syllogism, carried to the Nine like a coin. The blood "did not run like blood but caught like oil." The ground under the city is "a single dark warmth threaded down through the bones of the world," and it remembered "what it had been called before the new law threw the old word away."

**Rulings.** First, blood: the fire is not something the wyrm breathes; it is what the wyrm is *made of*, and bleeding is how it burns. Wounding it makes more fire. Vessa's syllogism must be tested in the book and must fail this way — not because the thing cannot die, but because its dying is a burning, and **a burning leaves an egg**. To kill it is to hatch its successor in the ashes of the town that killed it. That makes Hedric's war the one guaranteed catastrophe, and the egg following the column not a mystery but a *sentence*.

Second, the ground. Take the slow, unpreventable transformation and apply it to the seam itself: the "older burning" is a wyrm that slept so long it stopped being a creature and became a *place* — aware of what was being done to it, unable to prevent it. The Free Cities were built on it. Its note is the lowest in the chord, and it is what Peryn's lost book was about. You need not say any of this; you need only know it, so that when the ground answers in Chapter Five it answers in character.

## The consistency ledger

Fixed by Chapters 1–4; no ruling above may contradict it:

- It bled, and the blood burned like oil. It spoke three words: an introduction. At the breach it flew *toward* the cage — into the country, "into the arms of the only people organized enough to build a chain," not north to "the old high places its kind were said to keep." "Every account keeps that."
- Only after the collar closed did it walk: eleven days at ox-pace, never strained the chain, never ate, never spoke again.
- The egg: man-sized, ridged, dark, translucent at the crown, warm, singing one note; it moves unseen; it has *stirred* but not hatched; it feels "older" than the wyrm.
- The doors barred with no hand on them. The bar's note and the egg's note are one note.
- The hill-wyrm has "true" wings, came "without hurry," circled "once, twice, slow and enormous and high," settled, and waits with "the same terrible patience" as the first. Its face is undescribed.
- Nobody living knows the three words, the old word, or the book.

## What not to decide

Do not decide the three words. Do not decide whether *god* is right. Do not decide the egg's parentage in the narration — decide it here, then let "some said" and "some swore" carry it. The reference states its lore flat, because a bestiary must. Your chronicle has the better instrument: it can offer three folk-beliefs about what a wyrm is and let the action confirm the one nobody voted for.
